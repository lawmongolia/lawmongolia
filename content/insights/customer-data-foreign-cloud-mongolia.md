Modern businesses increasingly rely on foreign technology
infrastructure. Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, Salesforce, HubSpot,
AWS and Azure have become ordinary business tools for many Mongolian
companies.

Their use, however, should not be treated solely as an IT procurement
decision.

Once identifiable information - such as a customer's name, telephone
number, email address, location, delivery address, purchasing history or
employee records - is placed in a foreign system, the arrangement also
becomes a personal-data compliance matter.

## Apparently ordinary information may still be personal data
Personal data is not limited to a national registration number or
identity-card details.

A person's name, address, telephone number, email address, location,
user account and other information capable of identifying that
individual directly or indirectly may fall within the scope of personal
data.

A CRM record containing only a name, telephone number and email address
should therefore not be assumed to fall outside the law.

Health, genetic, biometric and communications data are among the
categories that require a higher level of protection.

## Outsourcing storage does not outsource the controller's responsibility
Where a Mongolian company decides which customer information to collect
and for what purpose it will be used, the company will generally act as
the data controller.

A cloud, CRM or SaaS provider that stores or processes the information
on the company's instructions may act as a data processor.

A controller may contract with a processor to perform processing
activities, but doing so does not eliminate the controller's underlying
obligations to the data subject.

Accordingly, saying that "the technology vendor caused the incident"
will not necessarily resolve the Mongolian company's own legal exposure.

Vendor selection, the terms on which data was transferred, the security
obligations imposed by contract, and the controller's own oversight may
all be relevant.

## International transfer is a separate compliance issue
When customer information is processed through a foreign system, an
important first question is where, and to whom, the data is being
transferred outside Mongolia.

Mongolian personal-data law imposes specific requirements on transfers
to persons, legal entities and international organisations located
abroad.

It is therefore not enough to look only at the physical location of a
server.

A company should also identify the legal entity providing the service,
the persons or entities able to access the data, any subprocessors used
by the provider, and the jurisdictions through which the data is
processed.

## A privacy policy alone is not enough
Some businesses approach data compliance by publishing a privacy policy
on their website and treating the matter as complete.

A policy, however, provides little protection if the company's actual
processing activities do not match what the document says.

Where consent is required, the individual should receive clear
information about what data is collected, why it is used, how long it
will be retained, whether it will be disclosed to others, to whom it may
be disclosed, and how consent may be withdrawn.

A generic statement that information "may be shared with third parties
to improve our services" may therefore fail to describe the company's
real data flows with sufficient clarity.

What matters is that the company itself understands who receives the
information, for what purpose and on what legal basis.

## One SaaS product may involve several other companies
Employees may think that the business uses only one CRM provider.

In reality, the CRM may host its infrastructure with another cloud
provider, outsource email delivery to a separate company, and use yet
another vendor for customer support.

A single customer record may therefore pass through systems operated by
several different legal entities.

For that reason, businesses should review not only the main service
agreement but also the data processing agreement, the subprocessor list,
relevant data locations, security commitments and breach-notification
terms.

## The data processing agreement should be specific
When contracting with a foreign technology provider, price and service
functionality are only part of the legal review.

The agreement should address what information will be processed and for
what purpose, how long it will be retained, where it may be stored, who
may access it, how it will be secured, how long backups remain
available, and what happens to the data when the service ends.

The parties should also establish how quickly the provider must notify
the Mongolian company of a personal-data incident.

Because Mongolian law may require prompt action following a breach, a
vendor's standard term providing notification only within 30 days may be
commercially and legally inadequate.

## Data may remain after the service contract ends
Ending a technology service does not necessarily result in immediate and
complete deletion of the data.

Even after deletion from a live database, copies may remain for a period
in backups, archives, logs or disaster-recovery environments.

The contract should therefore address return and deletion of data, the
period within which residual copies will be removed, and the method by
which deletion can be confirmed.

These points are particularly important where the system contains
sensitive information or large volumes of customer data.

## A data breach creates notification obligations
Where a processor becomes aware of a breach, it should promptly inform
the controller.

If the incident creates a risk of harm to the rights or legitimate
interests of the data subject, the controller may also have an
obligation to notify the affected individual.

This is why data security cannot be treated merely as an internal IT
issue.

A significant incident may require simultaneous involvement from legal,
compliance, customer service, communications and information-security
teams.

A documented incident-response procedure should therefore exist before a
breach occurs, rather than being improvised afterwards.

## Why data mapping matters
A practical first step in privacy compliance is to map the company's
actual data flows.

The business should be able to identify what information it collects,
where it is stored, who can access it, which foreign vendors receive it
and how long it is retained.

This exercise is commonly referred to as data mapping.

Without an accurate data map, it is difficult to assess whether the
privacy policy reflects reality, whether user consents are sufficient,
or whether vendor contracts contain the necessary protections.

## Conclusion
Using foreign cloud, CRM and SaaS systems is a normal feature of modern
business.

Once customer data is placed in those systems, however, the technology
decision becomes directly connected to personal-data law.

A company should know where its data goes, to whom it is disclosed and
for what purpose; establish the required legal basis and contractual
protections; impose appropriate security obligations on vendors; and
maintain the capability to respond quickly if an incident occurs.

Placing customer information in a third-party system does not fully
transfer the controller's responsibility to the vendor. On the contrary,
the controller's obligations to select, contract with and oversee its
providers become even more important.
