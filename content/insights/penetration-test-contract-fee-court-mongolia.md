Disputes over cybersecurity service contracts rarely concern technical findings alone. Equally consequential are the criteria for accepting deliverables, the calculation of fees, the formal amendment of commercial terms and the evidence needed when the parties disagree. A Mongolian civil judgment involving a penetration-testing assignment illustrates how these issues can determine whether a contractor is entitled to its full agreed fee.

On 11 May 2020, the Civil Court of First Instance of Sukhbaatar District, in judgment **181/ШШ2020/01247**, ordered a customer to pay **MNT 10 million** for cybersecurity work. The controversy was not simply whether a penetration test had taken place. It concerned whether the delivered reports met the contract, whether the price had subsequently been reduced to MNT 3 million and whether the customer had raised defects through the proper process.

## What did the cybersecurity contract require?

On 22 February 2019, two companies entered into a contract for work. The service provider was to examine the customer's website, mobile application, network vulnerabilities and system security, and deliver reports describing the identified issues. The agreed total remuneration was MNT 10 million, with staged payment provisions in an annex tied to milestones and findings.

The payment terms dealt with initial, medium-level and high-level vulnerabilities, including progress payments as certain categories of findings emerged. The contract also contemplated settlement of the entire outstanding fee upon delivery of the final report. According to the court record, the provider submitted reports on 4 March and 11 April 2019. The materials before the court described testing across the web, mobile and network environments and technical vulnerabilities identified in those systems.

The customer nevertheless disputed the amount payable. Its representatives said that some reported weaknesses were already known and questioned the severity classification of the findings. They stated that the parties had discussed valuing the completed assignment at MNT 3 million instead. Email correspondence and a proposed completion record were relied upon as proof of this revised valuation.

## Does negotiating a lower price amount to changing the contract?

The dispute turned on a distinction between **exchanging proposals about a fee reduction** and **concluding an enforceable variation of the original agreement**. The court found that the parties had communicated about lowering the price of the previous work to MNT 3 million, apparently in the context of possible further cooperation. However, it concluded that the evidence did not establish a legally effective amendment reducing the original MNT 10 million obligation.

The judgment referred to provisions of the Civil Code concerning electronic expressions of intent and the formation of written contracts, including articles 43.2.4 and 196.1.2 as they applied to the case. It would be incorrect to extend this historical ruling into a claim that contractual amendments made by email are always invalid under present-day Mongolian law. The applicable legislation, the time of the transaction, the amendment formalities stipulated by the parties, the authority of those communicating and the substance of their agreement must all be assessed in the individual case.

The more durable lesson is evidentiary. Commercial discussions, an offer to compromise and a signed amendment are not interchangeable. Where parties truly agree to reduce a contracted fee, they should establish exactly which deliverables are covered, who approved the change, when it takes effect and whether any remaining payment claims are released. A complete documentary record is particularly important if discussions occur at project-manager level while final approval rests elsewhere in the organisation.

## A complaint about defective work must be raised in the appropriate manner

The customer also questioned the severity classifications and the quality of the reported work. The court attached weight, however, to the undisputed delivery and receipt of the reports and to the absence of a complaint about defects made within the applicable period.

Article 343.1 of the Civil Code provides the basic structure of a contract for work: the contractor performs the agreed work and the customer accepts the result and pays the agreed remuneration. The court also considered article 346.1 concerning when payment becomes due, and article 349.1 on the period for asserting complaints about defects. It reasoned that the customer had an opportunity to make a timely claim if the delivered work was incomplete or defective, but had not done so.

That does not establish an automatic rule that receiving any report makes every aspect of a technology service satisfactory. Acceptance requirements, the discoverability of defects, contractual warranties, statutory complaint periods and the available evidence may all change the result. On the record of this particular dispute, the court determined that the fee remained payable in full.

## What technology businesses should establish before signing

The distinction between 'work performed' and 'work accepted' requires careful drafting in software development, API integration, infrastructure testing, cybersecurity reviews and other specialist services. A contract describing only a 'penetration test' may leave too much unsaid about the systems in scope, the testing environment, the methodology, the number of testing cycles, the classification of findings and the minimum contents of the final deliverable.

An effective agreement should connect technical criteria with the commercial process. It should identify how and when a report is delivered, who may approve it, how long the customer has to raise specified defects, what remediation or retesting is available, and when each payment instalment becomes due. This is not simply a formal drafting exercise. It prevents a disagreement about a technical finding from turning into an open-ended debate about the entire contract price.

Equally important is amendment governance. Technical teams and procurement managers routinely discuss alternative scopes and prices during a project. If the agreement requires a written amendment or approval by a named officer, routine emails should not be assumed to satisfy that requirement without checking their contents and authority. For contractors, preserved reports, dated delivery records and properly approved amendments protect fee recovery. For customers, timely and specific defect notices preserve their position much more effectively than an unrecorded objection expressed only after a payment dispute emerges.

## The legal significance of the decision

The court held that the original contract for work was valid, the work reports had been delivered, a valid reduction of the price to MNT 3 million had not been established and no relevant timely defect complaint had been made. It therefore awarded the full **MNT 10 million** to the contractor. This case note addresses the first-instance judgment; the outcome of any subsequent appeal has not been verified.

The broader significance is not that a court guarantees payment for any technology report. It is that acceptance evidence, complaint procedures and the formal documentation of agreed changes can be as important to the legal outcome as the underlying technical work.
