Mongolia’s regulatory framework for virtual asset transfers entered a more operational phase in 2026. Resolution No. 258 of the Financial Regulatory Commission, dated 25 June 2026, introduced specific transfer-information requirements that became applicable from **1 September 2026**.

The significance of the reform is that customer identification is no longer confined to onboarding. For qualifying transfers, a virtual asset service provider must connect the transaction itself with identifiable originator and beneficiary information, transmit that information securely to the relevant counterparty and retain it in a form that can be produced to competent authorities.

## The MNT 3 million threshold

Where a virtual asset transfer has a value of **MNT 3 million or more**, the originating service provider must transmit information including the originator’s name or legal-entity name, registration number, transfer identifier or wallet address, registered address and purpose of the transfer, as well as information identifying the beneficiary, the beneficiary’s jurisdiction and the receiving virtual asset service provider.

The receiving service provider must likewise obtain specified information connecting both sides of the transaction with the relevant transfer or wallet identifiers. The regulatory model therefore requires more than a blockchain address: it requires an identifiable customer to be linked to that address for the relevant regulated transfer.

## Transfers involving unhosted wallets are not outside the framework

The use of a wallet that is not maintained by a registered service provider does not eliminate the information requirements.

When receiving a transfer from an unhosted wallet, the receiving service provider is required to obtain the prescribed originator and beneficiary information. Mongolia’s anti-money laundering framework also recognises transactions involving unregulated providers and unhosted wallets as a relevant risk factor.

Accordingly, the fact that a customer controls a wallet independently should not be treated as placing the transaction outside customer-identification and risk-control procedures.

## Information must accompany the transfer

The required information is not intended to be reconstructed only after completion of the transaction. It must be transmitted securely immediately before or during the transfer.

Before exchanging the required information, the originating and receiving service providers are also expected to identify the counterparty service provider and assess relevant customer-identification, confidentiality and security arrangements.

The practical effect is technological as much as legal. A compliant transfer system needs to collect, validate and transmit the required data at transaction level and should be capable of preventing or escalating a transaction where mandatory information is incomplete.

## Counterparty due diligence becomes part of transfer compliance

The framework does not stop with identifying a provider’s own customer.

Before exchanging transfer information, the service provider must identify the counterparty provider and assess whether the relevant customer-identification and information-security requirements are adequately addressed. Risk-based controls also require providers not to transfer assets to, or establish business relationships with, persons assessed as high risk.

As a result, inter-provider agreements, technical integration arrangements and counterparty-risk procedures become materially more important.

## Data collection does not remove data-protection obligations

Names, registration numbers, addresses and wallet information may constitute personal information capable of identifying an individual. The regulatory obligation to collect such information does not create an unrestricted right to use it for unrelated purposes.

Mongolia’s Law on the Protection of Personal Information applies to the collection, processing, use and security of personal information through software and technical systems.

Virtual asset service providers therefore need to satisfy two legal objectives simultaneously: collecting and retaining information required by financial and anti-money laundering regulation, while protecting that information against unauthorised access and use.

## Practical implications for systems and contracts

The reform should be reflected in both system architecture and legal documentation. Inter-provider arrangements should clearly address the data fields to be exchanged, timing of transmission, responsibility for verifying data, treatment of incomplete information, secure transmission, record retention and responses to requests from competent authorities.

The current operating requirements also call for at least two-factor authentication when transferring customer virtual assets and verification of relevant IP-address and wallet-address information.

## Conclusion

The 2026 framework demonstrates a clear regulatory shift from a model based primarily on wallet addresses toward one based on **wallet identity, verified customer information and traceable transfer records**.

For service providers, this is not merely an additional form or compliance field. It requires coordinated changes to customer due diligence, data protection, counterparty assessment, technology architecture and contractual arrangements.
