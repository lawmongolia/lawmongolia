When an employee leaves a business, they may continue working in the same industry, attend familiar professional events or remain in contact with people they first met during employment. An employer may regard some of those activities as solicitation or misuse of confidential information. But is a fixed contractual penalty automatically recoverable whenever the employer suspects a breach? A Mongolian first-instance judgment involving a **MNT 20 million claim** shows why the contractual wording and the proof of the alleged conduct must be examined separately.

## The employer's MNT 20 million claim

A company had entered into an employment agreement and a separate confidentiality agreement with an employee. Following the end of the employment relationship in April 2024, the employer alleged that the former employee had encouraged agents associated with the business to move elsewhere and had used information obtained during employment for another organisation's benefit. The employer sought a declaration that several contractual provisions had been breached and payment of the agreed MNT 20 million penalty.

The employer referred to witness testimony, photographs, contact with industry participants and the employee's earlier access to business information. The former employee disputed that any identified confidential information had been disclosed or that specific agents had been persuaded to change their commercial relationships.

The court **dismissed the claim in full**. Its central finding was that the employer had not established the alleged breaches through sufficient evidence. Importantly, the judgment did not declare that every confidentiality agreement or contractual penalty in an employment relationship is unenforceable. It concerned the proof and legal basis of the obligations invoked in that particular dispute.

## A penalty clause does not prove that a breach occurred

Employers sometimes adopt a fixed payment clause in the hope that its stated amount will provide an effective deterrent and simplify later litigation. A penalty clause may be relevant to the legal consequences of an established breach. It cannot by itself establish what a former employee did, which provision was breached or whether the provision can lawfully be applied.

Those are separate questions. First, what was the employee's substantive obligation? Second, does the obligation comply with applicable employment law? Third, is there evidence of the conduct alleged? Finally, if liability exists, what remedy may lawfully be claimed under the particular agreement and legislation?

Article 74 of Mongolia's Labour Law addresses duties concerning confidential information. It does not follow that every fact learned at work becomes a trade secret merely because an employer labels it confidential. A defensible arrangement distinguishes protected information from public information, ordinary professional knowledge and skills retained by the employee.

For example, a publicly available list of industry contacts is not necessarily equivalent to confidential pricing, client-specific terms or an unpublished strategy. Nor does the fact that a former employee attended an industry meeting necessarily demonstrate that they used or disclosed protected material. Conversely, evidence of unauthorised disclosure or exploitation of genuinely protected information may support a legal claim even after the employment relationship has ended.

## Confidentiality, non-competition and non-solicitation have different functions

Three distinct obligations are often placed into one broadly drafted clause. Confidentiality concerns the disclosure or use of particular protected information. A non-competition restriction may limit certain competing activities, subject to the conditions and restrictions imposed by labour law. Non-solicitation provisions address attempts to divert clients, employees or commercial agents.

Calling all three restrictions 'confidentiality' does not make their legal effects identical. Duration, scope, protected interests, contractual consideration where relevant, and the employee's ability to work must be assessed according to the actual substance of the restriction. A clause purporting to keep information confidential indefinitely should not be treated as an unrestricted prohibition on future employment.

In the reported case, the defendant also challenged a contractual restriction against working for a similar business for two years. That argument was part of the parties' dispute; the decisive ground on which the reported first-instance judgment rejected the claim was the absence of adequate proof of the breaches alleged. It would therefore overstate the decision to present it as a blanket ruling invalidating every comparable restraint.

## What evidence is useful in an actual dispute?

A company is in a stronger position when its contracts identify protected information with reasonable clarity and its operational policies show how that information is controlled. Relevant evidence may include access authorisations, lawful audit logs, the issue and return of devices, the dates on which access rights were removed, and contemporaneous communications showing an actual transfer or use of identified material. Such controls must themselves comply with personal-data and privacy rules; they do not authorise unrestricted surveillance of an employee's personal communications.

For a non-solicitation allegation, the evidence should address conduct rather than suspicion. Who contacted whom? What proposal was made? What business relationship changed, and when? Did the former employee solicit that change or simply participate in an open industry event? Photographs and social connections may form part of the evidential picture, but they may not answer these questions on their own.

Employees also have practical reasons to preserve a clear departure record. Documenting the handover of work, the return of devices and the end of access privileges can reduce future disagreement. A former employee who uses information from a public source should be able to identify that source rather than leaving the provenance of the information uncertain.

## Conclusion

The case is not an argument against confidentiality protection. It demonstrates the limits of treating a large penalty as a substitute for a carefully defined obligation and credible evidence. A company that can explain exactly what information was protected, how the employee was bound, what conduct took place and how that conduct breached a lawful restriction is better positioned than one relying on a broad clause and suspicion alone.
