An employer and employee may agree to restrictions on working for a competitor after employment ends, but **such restrictions cannot be imposed on every category of employee**. [Article 72 of Mongolia's Labour Law](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/16230709635751) regulates post-employment non-compete obligations for a defined category of employees, limits their duration to one year and requires monthly compensation of at least 50% of the employee's last monthly remuneration. Writing “the employee cannot work in this industry for three years” does not make that restriction enforceable simply because the contract has been signed.

## Which employees can be bound by a non-compete obligation?

Article 72.1 allows the parties to agree to a non-compete obligation with an employee who has a **special-conditions employment contract under Article 65**. The restriction is intended to protect production and business secrets and may cover employment with a direct competitor or carrying on directly competing activities after the employment relationship has ended.

Article 65.1 connects a special-conditions employment contract with an employee's exercise of specified aspects of ownership rights on behalf of the owner or the performance of executive-management-level duties. Consequently, a company-wide practice of requiring **all employees** to sign the same non-compete form does not automatically satisfy Article 72.1. The real duties of the position, the type of employment contract and the employee's role and access to business secrets should be assessed, rather than relying solely on a job title.

## What must the agreement specify?

Article 72.2 requires the contract or supplemental employment term to address the **reasons for prohibiting competition, the activities concerned, the territory covered, the duration of the restriction and the compensation payable**, among other conditions. An employee must be able to understand which activities are restricted, and the employer should be able to identify the legitimate business secrets it seeks to protect.

For example, a restriction providing that an executive in a software business may not work for “any technology company anywhere in the world” creates questions about whether the activity is truly directly competitive and whether the territorial restriction is appropriately defined. A clause identifying the relevant product market, competing business activity, territory and duration is more closely aligned with the specificity required by Article 72.2. Precision alone, however, cannot cure a failure to satisfy the law's other eligibility conditions.

## How long can the restriction last, and how much is payable?

Article 72.3 limits the duration of a contractual non-compete obligation to **one year after the end of employment**. A two- or three-year restriction therefore raises an issue as to the enforceability of the period exceeding the statutory maximum. The commencement date should be linked clearly to the termination of the actual employment relationship.

Under Article 72.4, while the restriction remains in force, the employer must pay the employee **monthly compensation of at least 50% of the last month's remuneration**. Assume, for illustration, that the employee's final monthly remuneration was MNT 4,000,000 and the parties agreed to a six-month non-compete period. At the statutory minimum rate, the monthly compensation would be MNT 2,000,000 and the aggregate for six months MNT 12,000,000. This is an illustrative calculation only. The actual components of the last month's remuneration and the individual agreement must be established from the evidence.

An employer that demands post-employment exclusivity but omits the compensation mechanism creates a substantial statutory-compliance problem. The parties should specify the payment schedule, calculation basis and means of documenting payment when agreeing to the restriction.

## Which workers cannot be placed under this obligation?

Article 72.6 **prohibits** imposing non-compete obligations on **minors, probationary employees and apprentices**. Article 72.5 also expressly provides that the obligation **does not apply when the employee works abroad**. These statutory limitations cannot simply be disregarded because a person signed a standard document before leaving the employer.

For the separate rules governing probation, see [probationary employment periods and pay](/en/insights/probation-period-extension-pay-mongolia-2026). A non-compete restriction must also be distinguished from a confidentiality obligation. The former restricts specified post-employment competition, whereas the latter concerns the use and disclosure of confidential information. Article 74 of the Labour Law regulates employees' duty to protect secrets separately.

## Is a training-cost repayment obligation the same as a non-compete?

Some employment documents combine a post-employment restriction with a commitment to remain employed after training paid for by the employer. These are legally distinct arrangements. Under **Article 73.3**, the period during which an employee agrees to continue working after employer-funded training must be mutually agreed and must **not exceed three years**. Article 73.4 separately addresses proportionate reimbursement of training costs where an employee terminates employment at their own initiative before completing the agreed period. These provisions do **not extend the one-year non-compete maximum** established by Article 72.3 to three years. It is therefore important to identify which clause concerns training expenses and which restricts competition, rather than treating them as interchangeable obligations.

## What needs to be proved in a dispute?

An employer alleging a breach must first identify why the worker fell within Article 65, whether the terms satisfied Article 72, whether the new activity genuinely competed **directly**, and whether the period, territory and compensation terms were observed. Evidence may include the special-conditions employment contract, the signed addendum, proof of compensation and reliable information about the activity allegedly undertaken.

The employee should retain the contract, the end-of-employment decision, payment records and accurate information about the duties of any new position. The fact that two employers operate in the “same sector” does not, by itself, prove the type of **direct competition** contemplated by Article 72.1. A dispute about a post-employment restriction must also be distinguished from an allegation of disciplinary misconduct during employment. The correct legal characterisation depends on when the conduct occurred and which obligation is said to have been breached.
