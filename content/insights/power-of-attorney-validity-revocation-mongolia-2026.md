A person may authorize somebody else to collect documents, deal with a bank, sign agreements or act before an organization. The practical questions are when that authority expires and how it can be withdrawn. **A power of attorney that states no expiry date is not necessarily valid indefinitely.** Mongolia's Civil Code prescribes time limits and protects certain third parties who are unaware that an authority has ended.

## A power of attorney and the authority to represent someone

Under [Articles 62.3, 63.2 and 64.1 of Mongolia's Civil Code](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail?lawId=299), authority to represent another person may arise by law or through an authorization. When an authorized representative concludes a transaction in the principal's name and within the granted powers, the resulting rights and obligations generally accrue to the principal. A power of attorney is therefore not merely a letter of introduction; it can enable transactions with legal consequences for someone else.

For example, authority to request records relating to an apartment does not automatically authorize the agent to sell or mortgage that apartment. The precise powers stated in the instrument, and the requirements applicable to the intended transaction, must be examined. Unnecessarily broad or ambiguous wording increases the risk of actions the principal did not intend to authorize.

## The three-year maximum and the one-year default

**Article 64.5** provides that a power of attorney granted for a specified period may be valid for **no more than three years**. When no period is specified, it remains valid **for one year from the date of issuance**. Inserting a five-year term into an instrument does not displace the statutory maximum. Anyone granting time-limited authority should expressly state the term.

For example, an instrument issued on 22 October 2026 without an expiry period is subject to the one-year statutory rule. If it expressly grants authority for six months, that shorter period applies. Once authority has expired, an agent should not continue using the previous instrument as though it had been renewed. The authorized task may also be completed before the stated expiry date, bringing the mandate to an end.

## Formal requirements for a valid instrument

**Articles 64.2.1–64.2.5** set requirements for a written or electronic power of attorney. The instrument must bear the principal's signature and its date of issuance; where a definite period is intended, that period must be specified. For a legal entity, the authorized officer must sign and the entity's seal or stamp must be affixed as prescribed. An instrument authorizing receipt, transfer or disposition of a legal entity's property also requires the accountant's signature in addition to that of the authorized officer. Notarial certification is required where the law specifically prescribes it.

Under **Article 64.3**, an instrument that fails to meet the requirements of Article 64.2 is invalid. **Article 64.7** further requires an electronically issued power of attorney to satisfy the applicable electronic-signature rules. Sending a name or photograph by email does not, by itself, establish compliance with those formalities. Conversely, it would also be incorrect to suggest that every power of attorney must invariably be notarized; the applicable legal requirements depend on the intended act.

## Can a power of attorney be revoked before it expires?

Yes. **Article 67.3** allows the principal to revoke the authority at any time and the representative to renounce it at any time. An agreement purporting to exclude those rights is invalid. A principal therefore need not wait until the end of a three-year term before revoking the instrument.

Revocation, however, requires attention to notice. **Article 67.5** requires notification to the representative or third parties directly or through an official means of communication capable of reaching them. **Article 67.1** addresses notice of changed or terminated authority and the consequences of failing to notify third parties. The principal should identify the banks, registries, counterparties and other persons who may have relied on the instrument, rather than assuming that an internal decision to revoke it is sufficient.

Crucially, **Article 67.6** protects a transaction made with a person who did not and could not know that the representative's authority had ended. Failure to deliver revocation notices may therefore leave the principal exposed to a transaction the principal no longer wished to authorize. A dated notice, proof of dispatch and evidence of receipt are useful safeguards.

## Other events that terminate representation

**Article 67.2** lists several terminating events, including the agent's renunciation, the principal's revocation, death or loss of legal capacity of the relevant person unless law or agreement provides otherwise, termination of the issuing legal entity's operations, completion of the authorized act and expiry of the mandate. Under **Article 67.4**, an agent who learns that the authority has ended must return the instrument to the principal.

Where an unauthorized person concludes a transaction in another person's name, **Article 68.1** makes the validity of the transaction dependent on the purported principal's approval. **Article 65.1** may also protect a third party who acted in good faith after the principal created the appearance of valid authority. Consequently, the outcome depends on more than the paper's expiry date alone.

## Practical steps and evidence

First examine the instrument's issuance date, stated term, exact powers and any notarial certification. Then give clear revocation notices to the representative and to organizations where the authority has been used. Retain copies, emails, dispatch records and acknowledgments showing when each person received notice. Where the instrument was notarized, it may also be necessary to clarify the relevant notarial record procedure with the notary.

If an agent acts beyond the mandate, after its expiry or following revocation, the principal should collect the underlying agreement, the instrument and all correspondence. Determining whether the transaction binds the principal requires consideration of the granted powers, notice and the counterparty's knowledge. These factual questions are central to the legal analysis.
