A commercial contract may specify a reasonable price and a clear scope of work yet still leave a business exposed if its **limitation-of-liability clause** is poorly drafted. A party might suffer substantial losses from a breach but discover that the contract limits recovery to a fraction of those losses. A proper **contract review in Mongolia** must therefore examine not only the price, deadlines and late-payment penalties, but also **which party bears which loss, and up to what amount**.

This is particularly important for software, IT infrastructure, logistics, supply arrangements and recurring services. The consequences of one serious failure may exceed several months of service fees. That does not mean liability caps are inherently unlawful or that unlimited liability is always commercially sensible. The objective is to allocate risk consistently with each party's obligations, control over the risk and commercial bargain.

## 1. What is a limitation-of-liability clause?

A liability cap is a contractual limit on specified monetary claims arising from a breach. For example, a supplier may propose limiting its aggregate exposure to the fees paid during the preceding 12 months. **That is a negotiation example, not a statutory liability ceiling under Mongolian law.**

A useful review begins with three questions. Does the cap apply to each incident, each claim or all claims in the aggregate? Is it calculated against the total contract value, fees actually paid or fees during a defined period? Which obligations or categories of loss, if any, sit outside the cap?

A clause that does not answer these questions may cause more uncertainty than it resolves. The meaning of "total liability" can become contentious when refunds, penalties, compensation and third-party claims arise together.

## 2. Damages, contractual penalties and refunds are different remedies

**Damages** concern the consequences of a breach and the required connection between the breach and the loss claimed. Article 227.3 of the [Civil Code of Mongolia](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/299) addresses expenses incurred, loss of or damage to property, and income that would necessarily have been earned if performance had been proper. Evidence of the amount and causal connection remains important.

**Contractual penalties** (*anz*, including certain delay penalties) are a separate mechanism. Civil Code Articles 232.4 and 232.6 impose a ceiling of 50% of the value of the unperformed obligation on the total contractual penalty and a daily ceiling of 0.5% of the value of the unperformed obligation for delay penalties. **These are rules about contractual penalties; they do not automatically cap every damages claim at 50%.**

**Refunds, price reductions and service credits** may have distinct contractual functions. Refunding part of a monthly system fee following an outage does not, without more, establish that the customer has been compensated for the cost of rebuilding lost data.

When a draft states that "all liability" is subject to one cap, ask whether it also captures agreed penalties, ordinary damages, repayment of an advance, service credits and indemnities arising from third-party claims. Treating all these concepts as interchangeable is a common drafting error.

## 3. Can two businesses freely agree a liability cap?

Article 189.1 of the Civil Code recognises contractual freedom within the boundaries of the law. Commercial parties can therefore negotiate the allocation of risks. **Contractual freedom does not displace mandatory legal requirements or the rules governing validity and standard terms.**

Article 202.1 addresses standard contract terms that are contrary to the principles of good faith and fairness and are detrimental to the party accepting those terms. Article 202.2 contains additional restrictions relevant to standard terms in contracts with individuals who are not carrying on business activities. Those consumer-oriented restrictions should **not be applied indiscriminately to every business-to-business agreement**.

The correct analysis of a pre-written clause such as "the supplier is not liable beyond the amounts paid" requires attention to the full agreement, the extent of actual negotiation, mandatory rules and the essential contractual obligations. The mere presence of a signed agreement is not a substitute for that assessment.

## 4. Which risks deserve separate treatment?

A liability-cap negotiation should identify the most important loss scenarios rather than assume that one number can adequately address every breach.

- **Confidential information:** would a cap based on a small monthly fee be proportionate to the potential disclosure of source code, pricing, strategy or trade secrets?
- **Personal data:** statutory obligations of a data controller or processor cannot simply be eliminated by a private allocation-of-liability clause.
- **Third-party intellectual-property claims:** who manages the defence, replacement licences, settlement decisions and associated costs if supplied software infringes another party's rights?
- **Intentional misconduct and serious fault:** whether a proposed exclusion or limitation is enforceable must be assessed against the applicable law and the circumstances, rather than assumed.
- **Service interruption and data loss:** how do service credits interact with necessary recovery work and provable financial losses?

These risks do not necessarily require unlimited liability. Depending on the contract, a higher dedicated cap, insurance, specific warranties, staged payments, an indemnity or an enforceable restoration obligation may be more appropriate.

## 5. Does excluding 'indirect or consequential loss' solve the problem?

International templates frequently exclude "indirect", "consequential" or "incidental" damages. Importing those expressions into a Mongolian-law contract without defining them can leave both parties unsure about what is actually excluded.

Consider lost revenue from a failed production system, the cost of engaging a specialist to restore data and refunds paid to affected customers. These are economically distinct loss categories. Merely labelling a loss "indirect" does not answer whether a particular claim is excluded or whether the claimant can prove it under the applicable law.

A more reliable drafting method is to identify **specific recoverable and excluded categories of loss**, while preserving the legal analysis required by Article 227.3 of the Civil Code. If English and Mongolian versions of the agreement are used, the defined terms should carry consistent meanings in both versions.

## 6. A practical negotiation example

Suppose a supplier operates a system that a customer uses to manage sales and customer data.

**High-risk wording:** "The service provider's total liability shall not exceed one month's service fee. The customer shall have no other claim." This formulation may place core service failures, confidentiality breaches, restoration costs, refunds and other remedies under the same narrow ceiling without distinguishing their nature.

**A more considered approach:** negotiate one cap for ordinary service failures, address confidentiality, data protection, third-party IP claims and refunds separately, and state expressly whether service credits are the sole remedy or may coexist with other claims in specified circumstances.

Neither formulation is a model clause ready for insertion into any contract. The appropriate terms depend on the price, risk profile, scope of service, available insurance and negotiating position of the parties.

## 7. Seven questions before signing

1. Does the cap apply to a single incident, to each claim or to aggregate liability over the contract term?
2. Does the fee base include VAT, additional services, prepaid amounts and payments from previous periods?
3. Are the parties subject to reciprocal limits, or is one party's exposure effectively unlimited?
4. How do damages, agreed penalties, service credits and repayment obligations interact?
5. Are confidentiality, personal data and intellectual-property risks expressly addressed?
6. Who controls notification, defence and settlement of third-party claims?
7. Which liability and evidence-preservation obligations survive termination?

## Conclusion

The right question is not simply whether a commercial contract contains a liability cap. It is **what the cap covers, which risks are treated separately and what workable remedies remain after a breach**. Addressing those issues during negotiation is generally more effective than trying to reconstruct the intended allocation of risk after a dispute has arisen.
