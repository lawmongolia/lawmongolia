A company's executive director ordinarily has authority to enter into contracts on behalf of the company. However, does the director's signature always establish that all necessary corporate approvals have been obtained?

Under Mongolia's Company Law, certain transactions require a separate corporate decision, particularly where the transaction involves a substantial proportion of the company's assets or specified securities issuances.

## The scope of an executive director's authority

Article 83.1 provides that executive management conducts the company's day-to-day business within the authority established by the company's charter and the agreement with its board of directors or, where there is no board, the shareholders' meeting.

Article 83.8 permits executive management to enter into contracts, conclude transactions and represent the company without a separate power of attorney within the scope of authority granted.

This authority should nevertheless be distinguished from the internal approval required for particular classes of transactions.

## What qualifies as a major transaction?

Under Article 87.1.1, a transaction involving the sale, acquisition, disposal or pledge of property or property rights with a market value exceeding **25% of the company's total assets shown in its latest pre-transaction balance sheet** may qualify as a major transaction.

The rule may also apply to several directly related transactions.

However, transactions connected with the company's ordinary principal business activities are excluded from this category.

A contract is therefore not necessarily a major transaction merely because its monetary value is high. The nature of the underlying property or rights, the market value, the company's balance sheet and the ordinary-course exception must all be considered.

Article 87.1.2 also includes certain share and securities issuances exceeding the prescribed 25% threshold.

## Who must approve a major transaction?

Article 88.1 requires a decision approving a major transaction to be adopted unanimously by the board of directors or, where there is no board, the shareholders' meeting.

Under Article 88.2, where the board cannot reach a unanimous decision, the matter is referred to the shareholders' meeting and decided by a majority of votes of the participating shareholders.

An important exception appears in Article 87.4: the major-transaction provisions of Chapter Eleven do not apply where a company has a single owner and that owner personally performs the executive management function.

The applicable corporate structure therefore matters.

## Corporate approval should be reviewed before execution

In practice, a commercial team may negotiate the substantive terms of an agreement before determining whether the company has completed the required internal approval process.

That approach can create avoidable uncertainty concerning the signatory's authority, corporate decision-making and the legal consequences of the transaction.

However, a missing internal approval does not automatically establish that every affected contract is invalid. The consequences must be assessed under the applicable rules governing transactions, representation and the specific circumstances of the parties.

## Contract review involves more than drafting

A sound legal review should address both the contractual terms and the authority to enter into the transaction.

Before execution, a company should determine the nature and value of the transaction, the signatory's authority, applicable restrictions in the charter, whether major-transaction approval is required and whether any conflict-of-interest requirements apply.

The agreement itself should then be reviewed for performance obligations, payment terms, liability, termination rights and dispute-resolution provisions.

Corporate decision-making and contract drafting should therefore be treated as connected processes.

## The role of ongoing legal counsel

Corporate legal risk does not arise only when a company enters into a major acquisition or financing arrangement.

Ordinary supply agreements, service contracts, leases, employment decisions and regulatory matters also require recurring legal judgments.

Ongoing legal counsel can assist a business with contract review, preparation of corporate decisions, assessment of delegated authority, legal compliance and early identification of potential disputes.

The appropriate arrangement will depend on the organisation's size, internal resources, transaction volume and legal exposure.

## Conclusion

A director's authority to represent a company is important, but it does not eliminate the statutory approval requirements applicable to major corporate transactions.

Articles 87 and 88 of Mongolia's Company Law establish a specific framework for identifying and authorising such transactions.

Before signing a material agreement, companies should therefore assess both **the substantive contractual risk and the corporate authority required to approve and execute the transaction**.

Effective legal oversight begins before a contract is signed, not only after a dispute has arisen.
