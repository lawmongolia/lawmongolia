# The legal classification of BNPL depends on who finances the deferred payment

Buy-now-pay-later (BNPL) products have become a practical way to spread the cost of goods and services over time. An arrangement may appear straightforward to the customer: the purchase is completed immediately and payment falls due later or in instalments. Legally, however, that customer experience can be delivered through materially different contractual structures. Instalment payments do not, by themselves, establish either that the product constitutes regulated lending or that it is exempt from financial-services requirements.

The legal review should begin with three questions. Who supplies the goods or services? When does the merchant receive the consideration, and from whom? Most importantly, which entity acquires the enforceable right to recover the deferred amount from the consumer? Those questions distinguish an ordinary deferred-price sale from third-party credit, receivables financing and other financial arrangements.

## A merchant's own deferred-price sale has a distinct contractual basis

Article 262.1 of Mongolia's [Civil Code](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail?lawId=299) recognises sale and purchase on credit: the seller transfers property before the buyer pays, while the buyer pays the agreed price in full at a later date or by instalments. Article 263 prescribes form and contractual content for this category. Therefore, a merchant delivering its own goods and accepting the price in instalments is not automatically in the same legal position as an independent financial institution disbursing a loan.

This is not a general exemption for any service described as merchant instalments. A business may be regularly funding third-party purchases, disbursing cash, acquiring receivables for a financing return or charging for the time value of money through a separate structure. When such features arise, counsel should examine whether another regulated activity is being performed. The legal assessment turns on substance and the parties' obligations, not merely the absence of the word “loan”.

There is also a distinction between the purchase-price obligation and a new financing obligation. If the merchant remains the creditor throughout the transaction, the analysis starts from the commercial sale. If a separate participant extinguishes that debt and establishes a new debt owed to itself, the analysis may instead turn to lending or another finance product. Evidence of where the money and contractual claims move is central.

## Third-party payment to the merchant can change the analysis

In another BNPL model, a technology company or financial institution immediately pays the merchant and then collects instalments from the consumer. The central legal question becomes whether the customer has received credit from that financier, or whether the arrangement is properly characterised as an assignment or purchase of the merchant's existing receivable.

Articles 6 and 7 of the [Law on Non-Banking Financial Activities](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail?lawId=103) address the authorisation of non-bank financial activity and identify lending and factoring among the regulated categories. The [Law on Deposits, Monetary Transfers and Lending Activities of Banks and Authorised Legal Entities](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail?lawId=16230554816671) separately governs the relevant terms and repayment obligations of authorised lenders. These frameworks may be important where a licensed NBFI is the contractual creditor, but their application cannot be established from an app screenshot alone.

A transfer of an existing receivable must also be distinguished from a new loan. Assigning a merchant's claim under general civil law is not necessarily identical to operating an ongoing factoring business. At the same time, routinely purchasing or financing merchants' receivables may raise regulatory issues. The transaction documents should specify who owns the debt, how the consumer is notified of any transfer, who handles returns and disputes, and whether the merchant and financier can both demand payment.

## Issuing a coupon does not determine the financing relationship

Some platforms describe the amount made available to customers as an “entitlement”, “limit”, “coupon” or “voucher”. These features can perform very different functions. A prepaid voucher purchased by a user may simply be a right to receive future goods or services under specified terms. By contrast, a platform may offer an unfunded purchasing limit, pay participating merchants when it is used and demand payment from the consumer later. The latter structure calls for a separate credit and payment-services analysis.

The relevant documents should explain the precise point at which the consumer becomes indebted, the identity of the creditor, the method of setting the amount due, any financing charge and the consequences of default. They should also address the legal effect of a cancelled purchase. A discount coupon and a receivable funded by another company should not be treated as equivalent simply because the same mobile interface displays both.

## Finance leasing cannot be created merely by renaming a deferred service fee

Finance leasing is sometimes proposed as an alternative legal label for BNPL. But the [Law on Financial Leasing](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail?lawId=447), particularly article 3.1, describes a structure in which a lessor procures or produces qualifying property in accordance with the lessee's instructions and grants possession and use of that property for payment over a specified period. The law also regulates the property that may be leased and the agreement's required content.

Deferring the fee for a taxi trip, courier delivery, educational course or other service does not, without more, satisfy the legal structure of a finance lease. A lease requires an appropriate subject matter and a real relationship involving its use, possession and ownership. Renaming a service-price debt as “leasing” does not create those elements. Where actual property is being financed, the appropriate sale, credit or leasing structure should be considered on its own facts.

## Contract terms and the technical payment flow should tell the same story

A BNPL review should encompass the merchant contract, customer terms, financing agreement, repayment ledger, payment APIs and refund processing. For example, if a merchant cancels an order but the lender's repayment schedule remains active, the resulting dispute may arise from a mismatch between legal rights and system architecture. Similar problems occur when merchant settlement is reversed but the consumer still faces an unchanged claim.

Where an authorised lender is involved, the rules applicable to lending also require consideration. Article 25.1 of the law governing lending by banks and authorised entities provides that a borrower may perform the loan obligation, wholly or in part, ahead of schedule. Repayment and refund logic in a BNPL product must therefore remain consistent with the legal nature of the relevant obligation. Contract drafting cannot be separated from how the platform actually allocates and extinguishes debts.

## Conclusion

There is no sound basis for treating every BNPL arrangement as an unauthorised loan or every deferred payment as unregulated commerce. The controlling factors are the underlying sale, the financing party, the ownership and transfer of receivables, the basis of the customer's debt and, where relevant, the subject matter of a finance lease. The simpler the product looks to consumers, the more clearly its legal structure should be documented behind the interface.
