A Mongolian company may enter into a contract with a business owned by its director or a major shareholder. Such a transaction is not automatically prohibited. It may, however, constitute a conflict-of-interest transaction and therefore be subject to specific disclosure, approval and pricing requirements under the Company Law.

## Who is an interested person

A shareholder holding 20 per cent or more of the company's common shares, alone or together with affiliated persons, as well as an authorised officer and their affiliated persons, may fall within the statutory conflict-of-interest regime. This applies where that person is the counterparty to the transaction, or is an authorised officer or major shareholder of the counterparty, among other connections.

A conflict of interest is therefore not a statement that someone is taking money unlawfully or deliberately harming the company. The special procedure applies from the moment one person is in a position to influence both sides of a transaction.

## Disclose, then step out of the decision

The interested person must disclose to the board of directors — or to executive management where there is no board — and to the auditor: the companies they own, the legal entities in which they hold office, their affiliated persons, and their interest in the proposed transaction.

That person is not entitled to participate in the decision on whether to approve the transaction. The company is also required to maintain records of its authorised officers and their affiliated persons.

This produces a simple governance principle: disclose your interest in advance, then do not vote on your own transaction.

## Who gives the approval

As a general rule, approval is given by a majority of board members who have no conflict in relation to the transaction. Where there is no board, the shareholders' meeting decides.

The board must also determine the market value of the property, rights or services involved, in accordance with article 55 of the Company Law. An explanation that "the price reflects the fact that it is the director's company" is not sufficient. The basis for the price is itself part of the governance record.

For a joint stock company, additional requirements apply in specified circumstances — for example, where the transaction value exceeds 25 per cent of the company's recorded asset value, the matter must go to the shareholders' meeting and be approved by a majority of non-conflicted shareholders.

## Breaching the procedure is more than an internal defect

Where a breach of the conflict-of-interest procedure causes loss to the company, the person at fault may be required to compensate that loss from their personal assets. A court may also treat a transaction concluded in breach of the statutory procedure as invalid.

The issue is therefore not confined to an incomplete set of minutes. On a substantial related-party transaction, the validity of the transaction, the director's personal liability and a claim for the company's loss may all arise together.

## How to document it in practice

The internal process does not need to be complicated. Take a declaration of any connection to the counterparty in advance, exclude the interested person from the vote, document the basis for the market price, and obtain the board or shareholder approval before the contract is signed. That covers most of the risk.

Run the same check on agreements that look routine — leases, consultancy, supply, loans, guarantees and asset sales — not only on large intra-group transactions.

## Conclusion

A conflict-of-interest transaction is not a prohibited transaction. It is a procedure designed to protect the company's assets by making the interest transparent, having it decided by people without that interest, and establishing the market price.

Where the counterparty is connected to a director, a shareholder or their affiliates, the right question to ask before signing is: who is supposed to approve this?
