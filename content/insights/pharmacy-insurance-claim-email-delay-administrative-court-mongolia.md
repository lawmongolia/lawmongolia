If a company submits an invoice to a public authority three days late, does it automatically lose the entire amount due for services already provided? A Mongolian administrative case concerning approximately MNT 25.9 million in pharmacy reimbursement demonstrates why the answer may depend not only on the deadline, but also on how the prescribed communication channel operated and whether the delay had an excusable cause.

On 11 March 2025, the Administrative Court of Appeal issued judgment **221/МА2025/0179**, upholding a first-instance decision that found a public body's refusal to reimburse a pharmacy unlawful. Importantly, the courts did not simply order an immediate transfer of **MNT 25,906,031**. They required the agency to issue a decision providing for reimbursement in accordance with the applicable legal procedure.

## How the reimbursement dispute arose

The claimant, identified in the published judgment as “M B” LLC, operated a licensed pharmacy and had entered into an agreement with Mongolia's General Authority for Health Insurance to supply medicines to insured persons at subsidised prices. For medicines dispensed in February 2022, it sought reimbursement of the price difference amounting to MNT 25,906,031.

The contract required monthly reimbursement invoices to be submitted by the fifth day of the following month through a contractually designated channel. An invoice bearing the date 2 March 2022 was subsequently sent by email on 8 and 9 March. The dispute therefore included a **three-day delay** in submission of the relevant information.

The public authority included the pharmacy among providers that had not delivered invoices within the prescribed period. In a letter dated 13 February 2024, it refused to reimburse the sum claimed for that month. The pharmacy challenged the refusal after a protracted history of correspondence and proceedings.

## Why did the change of official email address matter?

Two circumstances proved particularly important. First, the annex to the contract renewed in 2022 identified a new email address for receiving invoices. The pharmacy, however, received the finalised renewed agreement only on **2 April 2022**. This was after the reporting deadline for the February transactions had passed.

Second, inspection records indicated that access to the previously used official mailbox could not be restored by the responsible authority's staff until **8 March 2022**. The litigation thus raised questions about which address was operational, when the new address was properly communicated and whether an invoice sent to the older channel could fairly be treated as if the supplier had supplied nothing.

This is not simply an administrative technicality. When a public body changes the contractual channel for formal submissions, the effective communication of that change and the functionality of the previous system can affect the parties' rights. For suppliers, the case similarly illustrates the importance of keeping contemporaneous proof of what was sent, to which address, and when it was received.

## A deadline is not always the only contractual condition

Article 10 of Mongolia's Law on Health Insurance provides for agreements between the health-insurance authority and contracted service providers, including rules on payment and the parties' responsibilities. In this dispute, clause 2.4.4 of the parties' contract required invoices to be sent by the fifth of each month. Clause 2.1.7.2, however, permitted the authority to refuse financing for invoices delivered late **without an excusable reason**.

Another clause, 4.2, prescribed a deduction of **0.5 per cent of the relevant invoice amount for each day of delay** where the submission deadline had been missed for an excusable reason. The contractual scheme therefore distinguished between situations rather than assigning every late invoice the same consequence. Assessing whether the delay had an acceptable explanation was central to the way the agreement allocated risk.

The Administrative Court of Appeal held that the authority had refused reimbursement without examining, in the exercise of its functions, whether use of the old email address and the three-day delay were supported by an excusable reason. The refusal was therefore not justified on the evidence and the relevant contract terms.

The ruling does not eliminate reporting deadlines, nor does it establish a general entitlement to full payment for every late claim. It illustrates a narrower proposition: where a governing contract makes the reason for delay legally relevant, the decision-maker must assess that reason instead of applying an undifferentiated refusal.

## The jurisdictional history was also significant

Before obtaining relief in the administrative courts, the pharmacy had pursued the disputed amount through civil proceedings. An appellate civil decision addressed the administrative-law nature of the underlying contractual arrangement, altering the course of that litigation. The claimant then pursued the public body's refusal through administrative channels and ultimately the administrative judiciary.

The Administrative Court of Appeal also rejected the agency's submission that the claimant had missed the time limit for approaching the court, noting the continuous nature of the claimant's attempts to contest the disputed reimbursement. That procedural conclusion arose from the particular sequence of proceedings and should not be read as a universal rule that filing in the wrong jurisdiction always protects statutory appeal deadlines.

When a business contracts with a public authority, a contract's classification as administrative or private-law in nature can affect the forum, preliminary remedies, time limits and the legally available form of relief. Those questions should be considered before issuing proceedings, not only after a court has challenged its jurisdiction.

## Practical lessons for companies and public bodies

An invoice-delivery address, online claims portal or document-submission workflow may look like an operational detail. In reality, it can determine whether a supplier receives payment or is drawn into litigation lasting several years. Contract teams should therefore control changes to official notification addresses, record the effective date of amendments and ensure counterparties can verify that the new channel has become operative.

A supplier should retain complete sending and delivery records, copies of each invoice, acknowledgements of receipt, evidence of email-server failures and correspondence about changes to the contact details in an annex. It should escalate unanswered claims promptly and preserve evidence that it has continuously challenged a refusal.

For administrative bodies, compliance checking should not stop at comparing two dates in a spreadsheet. If the applicable contract expressly recognises excusable delay, a reasoned decision should examine the evidence of that delay, including whether the agency itself was able to access the required channel and whether the amended contractual instructions had been communicated in time.

## What the appellate court actually ordered

The Administrative Court of Appeal upheld the decision of the Administrative Court of First Instance dated 3 December 2024, No. 128/ШШ2024/0987. It maintained the finding that the refusal was unlawful and the direction requiring the authority to adopt a reimbursement decision under the applicable statutory procedure. This must be distinguished from a judgment directly documenting the payment of the MNT 25,906,031. The outcome of any subsequent cassation review has not been independently established for this article.

The case illustrates a broader principle in public-sector commercial relationships: when payment rules depend on proper notice, communication systems and the reason for a late filing, the authority's assessment must engage with those facts rather than treat the deadline as the entire legal inquiry.
