If a shareholder in a Mongolian limited liability company (LLC) agrees to sell shares to an outside investor, must those shares first be offered to existing shareholders? What happens if the outside buyer receives a lower price? These questions are governed not only by the proposed share purchase agreement but also by **Articles 5.3–5.11 of Mongolia's Company Law** and the company's charter.

## What is a preemptive purchase right?

Under [Article 5.3 of the Company Law](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail?lawId=310), **unless the company charter provides otherwise**, an LLC shareholder is entitled to purchase, on a proportionate basis, shares offered by the company or another shareholder, subscription rights and securities convertible into such shares before they are offered to a third party. The applicable offer price is the price proposed to the third party. This does not prohibit a shareholder from selling shares; it establishes a procedure under which specified persons receive the first opportunity to purchase them.

The rule is specific to **limited liability companies**. It should not automatically be applied to freely traded shares of a public joint-stock company. Nor should the default rule be assumed to govern where the LLC's charter lawfully establishes a different procedure. Reviewing the latest registered charter is therefore a necessary first step.

## Who must receive notice of a proposed sale?

**Article 5.4** requires a shareholder intending to sell shares to notify the **company**. The company must then notify **the other shareholders in writing or electronically**. Merely mentioning a potential sale verbally to one business partner is not a reliable substitute for the formal statutory notice process. Electronic notification is expressly permitted in the current provision, but records of dispatch and receipt still matter.

Under **Article 5.5**, the notice must identify the class and number of shares being offered, the sale price, the number each shareholder may acquire under the preemptive right, and the applicable time limit and purchase procedure. A brief email stating only "I intend to sell my shares" may therefore be disputed as inadequate. Equally important is evidence that the relevant shareholders received the same operative information.

## How does a shareholder exercise the right?

**Article 5.6** requires a shareholder wishing to purchase under the preemptive right to notify the company of that decision **in writing or electronically within the prescribed period**. Under **Article 5.7**, the notification must state the buyer's name, parental name and address, the number of shares to be purchased, and include proof of payment. A casual message saying "I will buy" should not be assumed to satisfy every statutory condition.

**Article 5.8** allows a shareholder, unless the charter provides otherwise, to exercise the preemptive right in full or in part and to transfer it, in full or in part, to another shareholder. The resulting quantity calculations, payment records and any transfer of rights should be reconciled with the charter and the notice.

## How is the proportionate allocation calculated?

Consider an LLC with shareholders A, B and C owning 50, 30 and 20 percent, respectively. A proposes to sell A's entire 50-percent holding to a third party. If the charter does not provide otherwise and both B and C elect to exercise their rights, their existing holdings have a 30:20 ratio. For illustration, B would take 30 percentage-point units of the offered holding and C would take 20, corresponding to their relative proportions. The example uses simplified units; an actual transaction requires calculations by share number, class and the terms of the charter.

This illustrates a default proportional outcome, not an unqualified rule for every shareholder structure. If one shareholder declines to purchase or transfers a portion of the right, **Articles 5.8–5.9** must be considered in determining what happens next.

## The company's five-business-day decision

Under **Article 5.9**, unless the charter provides otherwise, a preemptive right not fully exercised by the shareholders within the prescribed period passes to the company. The company must decide whether to exercise that right **within five business days after expiry of the period stated in the Article 5.4 notice**. This is not a universally applicable statutory "30-day notice period" for every LLC share sale. The shareholders' response deadline must be checked against the actual notice and charter.

Before proceeding with a final transfer, the parties should establish whether the company made a decision within the relevant period and retain the supporting resolution and communication records. A missed meeting or unexplained silence may create avoidable uncertainty about whether the preemptive-right process was duly completed.

## What price may be offered to the outside purchaser?

**Article 5.10** provides that if the persons referred to in Articles 5.3 and 5.9 do not exercise the preemptive rights, the selling shareholder may sell the shares to a third party **at a price no lower than that stated in the original notice to the company**. For example, offering a share to existing shareholders at MNT 100,000 and then selling it to an outsider for MNT 70,000 would be inconsistent with that price condition.

Where a lower sale price becomes necessary, making a new offer on the revised terms and allowing the statutory preemptive-right process to operate again reduces the risk of a challenge. Negotiating with an outside purchaser or receiving an advance payment does not eliminate the rights of existing shareholders. Splitting the actual price into side agreements to obscure a lower effective price can create further legal and evidentiary risks.

## What should be checked before signing and closing?

The transaction documents should be checked against the current charter, shareholders' register, offer notice, proof of delivery, shareholder responses, payment confirmations, company resolutions and any applicable state-registration requirements. If a charter amendment is involved, **Article 17.9** makes registration relevant to its legal effectiveness. Regulated businesses may also be subject to sector-specific approvals that cannot be inferred from the general LLC rules alone.

**Article 5.11** independently gives an LLC shareholder the right to information about the company and access to its financial and other records. That right can assist a shareholder in evaluating the proposed price and transaction. Maintaining complete corporate records before closing can also reduce the prospects of a later disagreement about compliance with the share-transfer procedure.
