A clause stating that "a late-payment penalty of 0.5% shall accrue for
each day of delay" is common in Mongolian commercial contracts.

Yet inserting that sentence does not resolve every issue. Parties still
need to determine the amount on which the penalty is calculated, when
accrual begins, how high the total amount may become, and whether a
penalty may be claimed where the contract is silent.

The legal nature of the remedy should therefore be understood before the
clause is drafted or enforced.

## Distinguishing contractual penalty, fixed penalty and late-payment penalty
Under the Civil Code, the broader concept of contractual penalty is a
monetary mechanism used to secure or reinforce performance of an
obligation. It includes both a fixed penalty and a late-payment penalty.

A fixed penalty may apply where an obligation is not performed or is
performed improperly.

A late-payment penalty, by contrast, is specifically associated with
delay in performance.

It is therefore inaccurate to label every contractual breach as a
"late-payment penalty" event. A confidentiality breach, delivery of
defective goods, or performance of a prohibited act may call for a fixed
penalty, damages or another contractual remedy rather than a delay-based
penalty.

## The daily rate of 0.5% is a maximum, not a mandatory rate
The Civil Code limits a late-payment penalty to no more than 0.5% per
day of the value of the unperformed obligation.

This does not require every contract to use a 0.5% rate.

Parties may agree on 0.1%, 0.2%, 0.3% or 0.5% per day. A higher daily
rate stated as a late-payment penalty risks exceeding the statutory
ceiling.

Nor does the statutory 0.5% rate arise automatically in every contract.

If the parties have not agreed that a late-payment penalty is payable, a
creditor cannot ordinarily rely on the mere existence of the statutory
ceiling to claim 0.5% per day. A separate claim for proven loss caused
by delay may still be available, subject to the applicable legal
requirements.

## The penalty is calculated on the unperformed obligation, not automatically on the total contract price
One of the most common disputes concerns the calculation base.

Assume that a contract price is MNT 100 million. The buyer pays MNT 80
million on time, but delays payment of the remaining MNT 20 million.

The legally relevant base is the unperformed payment obligation of MNT
20 million, rather than the original MNT 100 million contract price.

At an agreed daily rate of 0.5%, the penalty would accrue at MNT 100,000
per day.

That calculation, however, does not continue without limit.

## The total contractual penalty may not exceed 50% of the unperformed obligation
The Civil Code imposes a second cap on the total amount of contractual
penalty.

The aggregate penalty may not exceed 50% of the value of the unperformed
obligation.

In the example above, the unperformed obligation is MNT 20 million, so
the maximum aggregate penalty would be MNT 10 million.

Even if a mathematical daily calculation would produce a higher figure,
the contractual penalty cannot exceed the statutory overall cap.

Two separate limits therefore operate at the same time: the maximum
daily rate and the maximum aggregate amount.

## The penalty agreement should be in writing
As a general rule, an agreement on contractual penalty must be made in
writing.

This is significant in practice. The existence of an underlying debt
does not necessarily mean that a separate penalty claim is enforceable
if the parties did not validly agree on the penalty itself.

A general phrase such as "the breaching party shall bear liability under
law" is therefore less useful than a specific clause.

For example, the contract may state that if payment is overdue, a
late-payment penalty of 0.3% per day will accrue on the outstanding
unpaid balance. This identifies both the rate and the calculation base.

## Why calculating the penalty on the total contract price is risky
In large contracts, most obligations may already have been performed
while only a relatively small balance remains overdue.

If MNT 490 million of a MNT 500 million contract has been paid on time
and only MNT 10 million remains overdue, calculating the penalty on the
full MNT 500 million would effectively impose liability on amounts that
were already duly paid.

A clause referring to the "overdue and unpaid balance" is therefore
generally clearer and less likely to create a dispute.

Where payments are tied to separate milestones, deliveries or stages of
a project, each payment should likewise be assessed by reference to its
own due date.

## A court may reduce an excessive penalty
A penalty that falls within the statutory cap is not necessarily awarded
in full in every case.

Where the amount is manifestly excessive, the court may reduce it after
considering the circumstances of the dispute.

Relevant circumstances may include the reasons for the delay, the
conduct of both parties, the size and duration of the obligation, and
the actual consequences of the breach.

This does not mean that a court will reduce a penalty simply because the
debtor considers it high. The assessment is fact-specific.

## Late-payment penalty and damages are different remedies
A late-payment penalty is a contractual remedy agreed in advance.

Damages, by contrast, compensate for actual economic loss caused by
breach.

Accordingly, the absence of an agreed late-payment penalty does not
necessarily prevent a party from claiming proven loss caused by the
breach. Such a damages claim, however, requires proof of the loss and
the causal connection with the breach.

## Conclusion
A well-drafted late-payment clause requires more than inserting the
number "0.5%".

The contract should identify the obligation to which the penalty
applies, the calculation base, the date from which it accrues, and the
applicable overall limit.

A clear clause not only supports payment discipline; it also makes the
calculation more transparent and easier to prove if a dispute reaches
court.
