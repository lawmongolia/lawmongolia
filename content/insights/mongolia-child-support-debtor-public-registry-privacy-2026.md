Child-support enforcement protects children’s everyday welfare. Mongolia has now adopted a provision requiring certain information about persons who fail to pay child support on time to be publicly available. The issue is not whether a parent can ignore a legally enforceable obligation. They cannot. The legal question is whether **public disclosure has carefully defined boundaries** that protect children as well as the people whose information appears in the registry.

## The 2026 amendment

The [Law Amending the Law on Enforcement of Court Decisions, enacted 3 July 2026](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail?lawId=17438790908582&showType=1), adds Article 47.3. The operative Mongolian wording makes information referred to in Article 47.1 publicly available for a child-support debtor who has not fulfilled the obligation on time.

The same amendment creates a separate reporting requirement under Article 44.2.18 when child support remains unpaid for **more than three months**. That provision concerns furnishing information to a credit information database. It must not be confused with the public availability of enforcement-registry information under Article 47.3.

The amendment is scheduled to operate when the revised Family Law enacted on 3 July 2026 takes effect, namely 1 January 2027. Thus, as of 30 October 2026, it is a prospective rule rather than an already-operating public disclosure measure.

## What information would be public?

[Article 47.1 of the Law on Enforcement of Court Decisions](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail?lawId=12701) refers to an electronic register of persons who have failed to comply with civil-court judgments. Article 47.2 delegates the procedure for maintaining the register and issuing related records and certificates to the competent minister.

Article 47.3 does not, on its face, enumerate all data fields that must be disclosed, identify every permitted audience or specify a publication period. Those points need to be determined from the governing implementing procedure and actual system design. It would be inaccurate to state, solely on the basis of Article 47.3, that addresses, identification numbers or children's identities must necessarily be made public.

## Child welfare and private information are not opposing goals

The state has a legitimate and significant interest in ensuring that court-ordered child support is paid. A carefully designed disclosure mechanism might support enforcement. It can also affect the privacy of a child who is not responsible for the non-payment.

Depending on the details made available, a parent’s publicly listed record could indirectly identify a child, family relationship, residence or other sensitive circumstance. That risk calls for separate analysis of the child’s best interests, as well as the debtor’s rights. The answer is not necessarily to abandon disclosure; it may be to limit its scope and duration.

[Article 16(13) of the Constitution of Mongolia](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail?lawId=367) protects personal and family privacy within its broader guarantee of inviolability and liberty. The [Law on Personal Data Protection](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail?lawId=16390288615991) supplies additional statutory context for collecting, processing and sharing information. These guarantees must be read together with enforcement legislation.

## Four safeguards that deserve scrutiny

**Purpose and scope.** Which specific items of information are necessary to make the child-support enforcement system work? Is any additional public disclosure justified?

**Correction and removal.** How quickly is information amended after payment, correction of an underlying judgment or identification of an error? Is there a meaningful procedure for challenging inaccurate entries?

**Access and retention.** Is the material available to everyone indefinitely, only to authorised users, or subject to meaningful limitations? What do the operative procedures actually say?

**Protection of the child.** Could the published material directly or indirectly reveal the child’s identity or household circumstances? What safeguards prevent unrelated adverse consequences for the child?

These are questions about implementation and proportionality, not a claim that the new rule automatically breaches the Constitution.

## Credit reporting is a different measure

The three-month threshold in Article 44.2.18 governs submission of certain non-payment information to a credit database. Public listing under Article 47.3 operates through a distinct legal mechanism. A credit report may influence future assessments by lenders, but the statute does not mean that every individual concerned is automatically disqualified from all borrowing. The precise data, reporting procedures and lender decisions must be considered separately.

## The constitutional question

A constitutional examination may focus on whether the scope of disclosure, available procedural safeguards and foreseeable effects are proportionate to the legitimate aim of protecting children and enforcing judgments. Article 16(13) and the equality guarantee in Article 14(1) provide relevant reference points.

No conclusion of unconstitutionality is asserted here. Nor can the absence of an existing Constitutional Court petition be certified through a general public search; the Court’s case-registration information requires separate confirmation.

A child-centered rule must be judged by whether it actually improves enforcement **without unnecessarily exposing the child or family to additional harm**. That is the right standard for a serious debate about the new law.
