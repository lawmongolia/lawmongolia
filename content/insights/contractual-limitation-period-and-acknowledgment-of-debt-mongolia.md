Does expiry of the statutory limitation period extinguish a contractual debt? What happens where the debtor subsequently acknowledges the obligation after that period has apparently expired?

The Supreme Court of Mongolia addressed these issues in **Decision No. 001/ХТ2026/00140 dated 28 May 2026**, arising from a contractual dispute involving outstanding consulting fees and penalties totalling MNT 108.6 million.

## Contractual performance claims are generally subject to a three-year limitation period

Article 75.2.1 of the Civil Code establishes a three-year limitation period for claims seeking performance of contractual obligations.

Under Article 76.2, the limitation period generally begins when the relevant right is infringed or when the claimant knew or ought to have known of the infringement.

In the case before the Court, the outstanding payment was due by 8 November 2021. On an ordinary three-year calculation, the limitation period would therefore have expired around 8 November 2024.

The claimant filed proceedings on 9 May 2025, making limitation one of the central issues in the dispute.

## Expiry of limitation does not automatically extinguish the debt

Article 82.1 of the Civil Code provides that, once the limitation period has expired, the obligor acquires the right to refuse performance.

Expiry of limitation does not, in itself, invalidate the contract or erase the underlying obligation. Rather, it gives the obligor a statutory defence against enforcement.

Conduct occurring after expiry may therefore affect whether that defence remains available.

Article 82.2 further provides that where the obligor acknowledges performance without knowing that the limitation period has expired, it may no longer refuse performance on the basis of limitation.

## Acknowledgment may interrupt the limitation period

Article 79.1 provides that limitation is interrupted where proceedings are properly commenced or where the obligor acknowledges the claim through an advance payment, payment of interest, provision of security or another form of acknowledgment.

A creditor’s unilateral demand should therefore be distinguished from conduct by the debtor acknowledging the obligation.

A demand letter by itself does not automatically interrupt limitation. The acknowledgment must arise from the obligor.

Partial payment, interest payment, a debt-confirmation document, an agreed revised payment schedule or provision of security may, depending on the circumstances, amount to acknowledgment.

## How did the Supreme Court assess the debtor’s silence?

On 14 February 2025, the claimant delivered a formal demand for the outstanding payment.

The defendant did not dispute receipt of the demand but did not respond within a reasonable period to reject the claim or state that performance was refused because the limitation period had expired.

Having considered the parties’ prior commercial relationship and the overall factual circumstances, the Supreme Court referred to the principle of acceptance by silence reflected in Article 196.2 of the Civil Code.

On the particular facts, the Court treated the defendant’s conduct as falling within the concept of acknowledging a claim “in another form” under Article 79.1.

This conclusion is fact-specific.

It does not establish a general rule that every unanswered invoice or payment demand constitutes acknowledgment of debt. The parties’ prior dealings, the nature of the demand, other conduct of the debtor and the surrounding evidence remain relevant.

## A court should not raise the limitation defence on behalf of a party

The decision also addresses an important procedural issue.

The Supreme Court observed that where the parties have not placed limitation in issue, applying it on the court’s own initiative would be inconsistent with the adversarial and party-driven nature of civil proceedings.

In the case, the defendant first raised limitation only after examination of the evidence had concluded and did not provide a sufficient explanation for the late objection.

A defendant intending to rely on limitation should therefore raise the defence clearly and at an appropriate stage of the proceedings, supported by a calculation of the relevant dates and evidence.

It is risky to assume that the court will identify and apply the limitation defence independently.

## Why did the acceptance certificate matter?

The underlying consulting contract had a total value of MNT 362 million. The customer had paid 80%, or MNT 289.6 million, leaving MNT 72.4 million of principal outstanding.

On 2 December 2020, the parties executed a contract-completion document confirming that the contractor had completed its obligations and delivered the relevant materials in electronic and paper form.

The customer later argued that the quality of the work was inadequate but failed to substantiate that allegation. The Court also took into account the absence of contemporaneous complaints concerning defects during performance, at acceptance or in the subsequent period.

A signed acceptance document is not necessarily conclusive in every contractual dispute, but it can constitute significant evidence that performance was accepted without reservation.

## Practical implications for creditors

The decision should not be understood as allowing a creditor to revive an expired claim simply by sending a demand letter.

The safer course is to monitor contractual payment dates, commence proceedings before limitation expires where necessary, or obtain a clear acknowledgment of the outstanding debt.

A debt-confirmation agreement, revised payment schedule, partial payment, interest payment or security may provide substantially stronger evidence than a unilateral demand from the creditor.

## Practical implications for debtors

Ignoring an old or disputed payment demand may also create unnecessary risk.

Where a business disputes liability or intends to rely on limitation, it should promptly verify the payment due date, calculate the limitation period, determine whether any part of the debt is admitted, and consider providing a written response that clearly preserves its legal position.

Ambiguous communications suggesting that payment will be arranged later, partial payments or offers of security may become relevant in a subsequent dispute over acknowledgment.

## Conclusion

Two principles arising from Supreme Court Decision No. 001/ХТ2026/00140 are particularly important.

First, **expiry of the limitation period does not itself extinguish a contractual debt. It provides the obligor with a defence against enforcement, and subsequent conduct may affect the availability of that defence.**

Second, **a court should not be expected to apply the limitation defence automatically on behalf of a defendant. The defence should be raised clearly and in time.**

The Supreme Court’s treatment of silence as acknowledgment depended on the specific facts of the case and should not be generalised to every contractual payment dispute.
