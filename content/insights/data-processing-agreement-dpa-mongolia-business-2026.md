A business outsourcing customer-data operations to a software vendor, cloud provider, customer-support team, payment integration provider or marketing company should not assume that a **non-disclosure agreement (NDA)** covers the necessary legal obligations. Keeping information confidential is only one part of the issue. The parties also need to decide what personal data may be processed, for what purpose, on whose instructions, by which subcontractors, in which countries and what happens when an incident occurs or the contract ends.

A **data processing agreement (DPA)** addresses those operational and legal questions. Under [Articles 19.1–19.2 of Mongolia's Law on Personal Data Protection](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/16390288615991), a data controller may entrust personal-data collection and processing to a processor under a contract, and that contract must address the purpose, term, categories of information and protection of data-subject rights. A well-drafted DPA turns those requirements into concrete duties rather than boilerplate confidentiality language.

## 1. Identify who controls the purpose and who performs the processing

The analysis starts with the actual information flow, not the labels chosen in the agreement. The organisation deciding why customer information is collected and used may be acting as the controller. A supplier handling the data on that organisation's instructions may be acting as a processor.

For example, a digital platform might choose to collect order information and hire a provider to host it and send notifications. But a supplier that independently reuses the data for its own products or marketing may be performing activities beyond the original processing instructions. The contractual label “processor” does not resolve the legal nature of every operation.

The same organisation can play different roles for different sets of data. Each material processing activity should be mapped by purpose, data category, lawful basis and responsible party before the DPA is finalised.

## 2. An NDA is not a substitute for a DPA

An NDA typically addresses secrecy, permitted disclosure, confidentiality periods and contractual remedies. A DPA needs to address **processing instructions, duration, data categories, data-subject rights, technical security, incident cooperation, subcontracting and return or deletion**.

A general statement that both parties must “keep all information confidential” does not establish whether cloud infrastructure may move to another country, whether an external support team may access production data or who must respond to a person's request to correct or remove information.

A DPA may be a schedule to the main service agreement rather than a separate standalone document. What matters is whether the agreed terms actually meet the law and operate consistently with the main contract.

## 3. Define the processing purpose, scope and instructions

[Article 19.2](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/16390288615991) expressly calls for the purpose of processing, contract duration, a list of information and measures protecting data-subject rights. Instead of authorising “all customer data for business purposes”, the parties should identify relevant categories such as names, contact details, transaction records, location information or device identifiers where applicable.

Processing instructions should state what the supplier is permitted to do, how the controller may update those instructions and what happens if the instructions conflict with applicable law. The agreement should distinguish processing necessary to deliver the contracted service from separate uses, such as training the supplier's own AI systems or conducting independent promotional analysis.

Such additional uses should not be assumed lawful simply because the supplier has technical access. They may require a separate assessment of the lawful basis and data-subject rights.

## 4. Allocate responsibility for access, rights requests and oversight

Under [Article 19.4 of the Personal Data Protection Law](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/16390288615991), processors have duties connected with following the controller's instructions, correcting or deleting information as directed and supplying the controller with necessary information. The DPA should make these duties operational.

Identify who receives a data-subject request, how it is verified, which team can locate relevant records and who authorises correction, deletion or restriction. Set a process for communicating requests between the controller and processor and for preserving the evidence of compliance.

Access controls matter as much as contractual promises. The organisation should know who can reach personal data, with which privileges, whether access is logged and how permissions are revoked when a vendor employee leaves the project.

## 5. Control sub-processors and third-party services

A vendor may rely on cloud hosting, analytics, email delivery, SMS gateways, customer-support systems or additional engineering providers. Under [Article 19.3](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/16390288615991), unless the processing contract provides otherwise, the processor may not transfer its obligations to others, and the contract should address liability.

The agreement should therefore identify approved sub-processors, their functions and locations, the process for adding or changing them and the security duties passed down to each party. An approval mechanism should be workable for a cloud service that routinely changes infrastructure, but it should not leave the controller unaware of who handles sensitive data.

Outsourcing a task again does not by itself remove the supplier's contractual responsibility. Nor does appointing a processor eliminate the controller's statutory responsibilities to the data subject.

## 6. Overseas access and data transfers need a legal assessment

The location of a data centre is not the only relevant question. Support personnel abroad, offshore engineering teams and external APIs may also create an international access or transfer issue depending on the actual facts.

[Article 14.1 of Mongolia's Personal Data Protection Law](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/16390288615991) restricts transfers to persons or organisations in other countries, subject to exceptions provided by law or international treaty or the data subject's consent. This should not be simplified into a categorical prohibition on all foreign cloud services; the parties must determine **what data is transferred, to whom, on what legal basis and under which safeguards**.

A practical DPA should identify relevant transfer destinations, the procedure for changes, access records and any contractual restrictions on overseas subcontractors. Additional rules may apply to regulated financial institutions or other particular activities, so the general data-protection analysis may not be the entire compliance review.

## 7. Incidents, security and an exit process

[Article 20.1](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/16390288615991) requires relevant security measures, including internal procedures, plans for responding to information loss, and protections for confidentiality, integrity and availability. A contract stating only that the supplier “complies with international standards” will not explain who investigates an incident, secures evidence, maintains logs or coordinates notifications.

The DPA should specify incident contacts, the first-notice process, cooperation duties, access to necessary technical records, containment steps, remediation and how the controller assesses external reporting obligations. A universal 72-hour statutory reporting rule should not be assumed without examining the applicable legal requirements and sector-specific regulations. The parties may negotiate their own contractual notification period.

Exit planning is equally important. Under [Article 19.4.5](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/16390288615991), when the purpose of processing has been achieved, the processor has obligations relating to return of the information and processing results, without retaining a copy, within the contractually specified time, and deletion that prevents further reuse. The parties should address export format, technical assistance, backups, deletion certification and any lawful record-retention exceptions that require separate treatment.

## Why the allocation of liability must be consistent

[Article 19.5](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/16390288615991) makes clear that a processor's breach of the controller's instructions does not itself release the controller from duties and liability to data subjects. A controller therefore cannot treat a one-line indemnity as a complete transfer of all regulatory risk.

The DPA should align with the main service contract on liability caps, reimbursement of response costs, third-party claims, confidentiality breaches and termination. Public-law obligations and contractual liability between companies are not the same thing. A poorly coordinated liability clause may leave the business exposed to costs that neither side intended to cover.

## A practical seven-point DPA review

1. Are the controller and processor roles supported by the actual data flow?
2. Are the purpose, duration, data categories and lawful basis identified?
3. Are instructions, oversight and data-subject requests operationally workable?
4. Are sub-processors and international access appropriately controlled?
5. Are technical security and incident-response responsibilities measurable?
6. Can the customer retrieve its data and verify deletion at contract end?
7. Do the DPA and main service agreement allocate liability consistently?

## Conclusion

A **data processing agreement is not a formality**. It connects day-to-day data handling, vendor oversight and the rights of individuals to enforceable obligations. The most useful starting point is a data-flow map showing what information is used, who uses it and why. The legal provisions can then be implemented through precise instructions, technical controls and contractual remedies.
