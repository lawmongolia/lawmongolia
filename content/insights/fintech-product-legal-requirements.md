Building a fintech product is not only a question of software, user experience and payment flows. Before it reaches the market you need to establish which regulated activity the product falls under, what authorisation it requires, what contract is formed with the customer, what data is collected, and how money is received and transferred.

Under Mongolian regulation, "fintech" is not itself a category of financial activity. Depending on how the product actually works, it may fall under non-bank lending, payment services, electronic money, money remittance or another regulated activity. The Law on Non-Bank Financial Activities and the National Payment Systems Law govern these separately.

## 1. Establish the product's legal classification first

The first question for any fintech product is what the activity actually is as a matter of law.

If money is advanced to a customer and repaid, the lending rules apply. If customer money is received and transferred to a third party, or a payment is executed, payment services regulation may be engaged. If customer funds are held in electronic form and can be used to make payments, the electronic money rules need to be checked separately.

Article 10.1 of the National Payment Systems Law provides for the Bank of Mongolia to authorise payment services, the issue of payment instruments, money remittance, electronic transfer of funds and the issue of electronic money.

Calling a product a "wallet", "instalment", "advance" or "account" does not change its legal character. Classification follows the actual flow of money, the rights and obligations of the parties, and the repayment terms.

## 2. Check licensing and product registration

Depending on the activity, authorisation will be required from either the Financial Regulatory Commission or the Bank of Mongolia.

A non-bank financial institution issuing a lending product digitally must hold a lending licence and, in addition, register the fintech lending product with the Financial Regulatory Commission. Under the current regulation, an NBFI issuing such a product must meet the requirements of ISO/IEC 27001 and hold paid-in capital of no less than MNT 5 billion.

Registration requires a product description, evidence that the requirements are met, and — where a third-party system is used — the relevant agreement, all filed with the Commission. Re-registration applies where the product is issued using new software.

Authorisation for payment services, money remittance and electronic money is granted by the Bank of Mongolia. An applicant must satisfy requirements on systems, internal control, risk management, the safeguarding of customer funds, and anti-money-laundering controls.

## 3. Give customers clear product terms

The contract, the terms shown in the application and the way the system actually behaves all need to be consistent with one another.

The regulation on non-bank lending requires the purpose, annual interest rate, term and other conditions of a lending product to be publicly disclosed, and requires the borrower to be informed of interest, fees, rights and obligations. The Law on Consumer Protection likewise establishes the principle that a consumer must be able to obtain accurate information about goods, work and services.

Before accepting the contract, a customer should be able to understand the total amount payable, interest, fees, the term, the repayment schedule, the consequences of late payment and the terms for ending the agreement early.

Where the information on the application screen differs from the contract, both a dispute risk and a regulatory risk arise.

## 4. If you are lending, assess ability to repay

Automating the lending process does not remove the requirement to assess a borrower's ability to repay.

The regulation on non-bank lending requires fintech lending products to take account of the debt-to-income ratio set by the Financial Regulatory Commission and the borrower's repayment capacity. Information on a fintech loan must also be reported to the credit information database once the agreement is concluded.

The product design therefore needs to settle in advance what information the lending decision rests on, how income and other liabilities are assessed, and how the basis for each decision is recorded in the system.

## 5. Process personal data in line with the law

A fintech product handles a customer's name, registration number, telephone, account details, income, transactions, location and much else.

The Law on Personal Data Protection applies equally to the collection, processing and use of data through software and technical means.

What data is collected and for what purpose, the legal basis for processing it, who it is disclosed to, how long it is retained, and what the customer is told about all of this should be settled at the earliest stage of product design.

Where facial recognition, fingerprints or other biometric data are used, the additional requirements applying to sensitive and biometric data need to be checked as well.

## 6. Treat information and cyber security as part of the product

Information security in a financial product is not only an IT matter. It is a regulatory requirement.

ISO/IEC 27001 is required for fintech lending products, and lending on the basis of data processed through a connection to a third party's IT system requires prior notification to the Financial Regulatory Commission. That third party must itself meet the requirements of the Law on Cyber Security.

The Law on Cyber Security establishes the legal basis for the integrity, confidentiality and availability of information, and transferring information security functions to another party by contract does not release an organisation from responsibility.

Where external software, cloud services, databases or credit-scoring systems are used, the contract should deal clearly with confidentiality, information security, continuity of service, the steps to be taken if data is compromised, and liability.

## 7. Implement customer due diligence and AML controls

Delivering a financial service digitally does not remove the obligation to know the customer.

A non-bank financial institution is a reporting entity under anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing regulation. The applicable rules require the money-laundering risk arising from a new product, service or delivery channel to be assessed, and the internal control framework to be updated accordingly.

An applicant for a payment services authorisation must likewise have an internal control framework for anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist financing.

Customer registration, due diligence, detection of suspicious transactions, sanctions-list screening and reporting therefore need to match how the product actually operates.

## 8. Keep customer money separate from your own

Where a product receives and holds customer money, the ownership, custody and refund structure of those funds matters a great deal.

The National Payment Systems Law requires a payment service provider to have arrangements preventing money received from customers for the purpose of executing payments from being used for any other purpose or mixed with the funds of others.

An issuer of electronic money must hold funds equal to 100 per cent of the electronic money issued in a guarantee account, and must convert electronic money back into funds at the customer's request.

Whether the "balance" a customer sees in the application is a liability of the company, electronic money, or funds held temporarily for the purpose of a payment therefore needs to be determined together with the contract and the actual flow of money.

## 9. Do not overlook the electronic contract and the evidence of it

Fintech contracts are concluded electronically in most cases. The Law on Electronic Signature establishes the legal and technical basis for applying a signature to electronic information.

Beyond that, it must be possible to prove later which version of the contract a particular customer accepted, when, and by what means. Retaining the contract version, the date and time of acceptance, the customer's authentication and the history of amendments reduces the risk in any subsequent dispute.

## Conclusion

Launching a fintech product starts with classifying the service correctly as a matter of law and identifying the authorisations and registrations it requires. The customer contract, product terms, personal data, information security, customer due diligence and the flow of funds are then built to match that regulatory position.

The initial legal assessment often has to be revisited when the product changes during development, because a change to the flow of money, the payment method or the relationship with the customer can change the authorisation, the contract and the regulatory requirements along with it.

This article reflects the regulation in force as at September 2026 and is provided for general information.
