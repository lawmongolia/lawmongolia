A software development agreement can expose a business to significant loss when **payment is due simply because another month has passed**, regardless of what the developer has completed. The project may be behind schedule, core features may fail in user testing, integration may remain incomplete and the developer may not yet have transferred source code or technical documentation. Yet the customer may already have paid most of the contract price.

The converse is also possible: a developer has delivered functioning software, but the customer refuses to sign acceptance documentation and withholds payment indefinitely. The more reliable approach is a **milestone-based contract** in which deliverables, testing, acceptance and payment are connected by objective procedures. This does not give a customer an unrestricted right to defer payment. Both sides need measurable criteria and a fair process to resolve disagreement.

## 1. Identify the legal and commercial nature of each obligation

A technology project may combine commissioned development, implementation, third-party software licences, cloud hosting, system integration and ongoing support. These obligations should not all be treated as if they were the same kind of deliverable.

Under [Article 343.1 of the Mongolian Civil Code](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/299), a contract for work generally involves the contractor performing agreed work and the customer accepting the result and paying the agreed fee. However, a composite technology arrangement must be assessed according to its actual obligations. A licence to use an existing platform, for instance, may require different acceptance and fee provisions from the delivery of a newly developed module.

The agreement should identify what is being **built**, what is being **licensed**, what is being **hosted** and what is being **supported**. Without that distinction, neither a payment schedule nor a completion certificate will necessarily reflect the underlying transaction.

## 2. A statement of work needs testable requirements

Descriptions such as “develop a mobile application” or “deliver a fully functional finance system” are too broad to establish whether the developer has performed. The statement of work (SOW) should set out features, user roles, interfaces, integration points, data flows, performance requirements, documentation, security obligations and supported environments.

The requirements should be capable of testing. For a payment integration, this may include successful transaction acknowledgement, prevention of duplicate processing, a defined refund flow, error handling and audit logs. For an enterprise application, access control, reporting and data export may be essential acceptance criteria.

Technical specifications are not merely an engineering attachment: they become **contractual evidence of what the developer promised to deliver**. Lawyers reviewing the agreement should therefore check whether the specifications are sufficiently concrete to support a later dispute.

## 3. Link each payment to a defined milestone

A milestone schedule can divide the work into stages such as requirements and system architecture, core features, integration, user acceptance testing, production deployment, code handover and warranty remediation. The contract may allocate a different percentage of the total fee to each stage.

The structure is illustrative; Mongolian law does not impose one mandatory milestone schedule on every software project. The critical issue is the **payment trigger**. “Thirty per cent is payable at the end of the second month” is different from “Thirty per cent is payable after the stage-two deliverables satisfy agreed acceptance criteria and the acceptance record is executed.”

A developer also needs protection against unreasonably delayed acceptance. The agreement should define the customer's test period, what constitutes a valid written rejection, how defects are addressed and when unresolved disagreements are escalated. A deemed-acceptance mechanism, if negotiated, should be drafted carefully rather than assumed to follow automatically from the customer's silence.

## 4. UAT and contractual acceptance are different

**User acceptance testing (UAT)** establishes whether specified user journeys and functional requirements operate as intended. **Contractual acceptance** records whether the deliverables have been accepted, subject to what outstanding items and with which payment consequences.

Software may pass its functional tests while the developer has not supplied repository access, deployment instructions, administrator credentials, security materials or agreed documentation. Conversely, minor visual defects may exist without preventing the customer from using the essential product.

A sound acceptance procedure distinguishes critical, major and minor defects, but these labels should be tied to agreed consequences. For each category, specify whether acceptance can occur, which payment is due, the remediation period, the retest procedure and how findings are evidenced.

## 5. Allocate responsibility for delays and failed testing

A contract should not treat every delay as a developer breach. The customer may have obligations to supply API credentials, test data, third-party approvals, access to infrastructure or timely decisions. The parties should record dependencies, delay notices and justified extensions.

When a milestone fails acceptance, the customer may negotiate the right to withhold payment attributable to the rejected deliverable while paying for undisputed work. This is different from withholding every invoice under the project. Any such mechanism should be consistent with the agreement's remedy, termination and dispute provisions.

A documented change log, test results, tickets, version numbers and contemporaneous notices can be more persuasive than later recollections. Both parties should agree on the systems and contacts through which these records become formal project evidence.

## 6. Distinguish a change request from fixing a defect

New business requirements are common during development. But a function that was promised in the original SOW and does not work is not necessarily a new chargeable feature. Equally, a new module that was never in scope need not be delivered free of charge.

A change-control procedure should require a documented request, assessment of additional price and time, authorised approval and an updated baseline. It should specify which persons may amend commercial terms and whether ordinary chat messages or project-management comments have contractual effect.

Without these controls, a project can reach a dispute in which the customer says the developer never completed the agreed scope while the developer says the customer repeatedly expanded it.

## 7. Source code, licences and operational access must be addressed separately

Acceptance of a working interface does not necessarily establish that the customer has received all intellectual-property or operational rights needed to maintain the product. A software handover may involve source-code copies, repository administrative control, deployment scripts, configuration records, documentation and licences for third-party components.

[Article 22.3 of Mongolia's Copyright Law](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/16230812154721) provides a default rule concerning exclusive exploitation rights in commissioned computer programs, unless the contract provides otherwise. That rule should not be treated as transferring all pre-existing modules or third-party licences automatically. Read the separate [software copyright analysis](/insights/software-copyright-employee-contractor-client-mongolia-2026) for the distinction between authorship, rights and possession of code.

Where relevant, the final milestone can require handover of the materials and credentials necessary for a suitably qualified replacement developer to continue operating the system. Access and IP terms should remain consistent with security obligations and any third-party licence limitations.

## 8. Warranty remediation and support after go-live

Production deployment is not necessarily the end of the developer's obligations. The parties may require a warranty period for defects, separate maintenance services, incident response, security fixes and updates to technical documentation.

The agreement should distinguish **warranty defects** from paid enhancements, set response and repair objectives by severity, identify exclusions and establish a workable escalation process. Whether a portion of the fee is retained during a warranty period is a commercial negotiation issue, not a universal statutory percentage.

A support service-level agreement is useful only if the measures can be verified: the clocks for response and resolution, what constitutes business hours, when an incident starts, acceptable evidence and remedies for repeated failures.

## A seven-question contract review

1. Does the SOW define measurable deliverables and dependencies?
2. Are fees tied to accepted results rather than the passage of time alone?
3. Who runs UAT, using which tests, and within what period?
4. Can critical defects prevent acceptance, and how does retesting work?
5. Who can approve additional scope, cost and time?
6. When are source code, licences, credentials and documentation delivered?
7. What happens to unfinished work, prepaid fees and customer data if the project terminates?

## Conclusion

A **milestone payment model** can protect customers from paying for incomplete work while giving developers a clear path to payment for accepted deliverables. The strongest protection is not a particular percentage retained or a lengthy clause: it is the practical alignment of **deliverables, evidence, acceptance decisions and payment triggers**.
