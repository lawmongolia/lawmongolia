A commercial contract does more than record what the parties agreed. It allocates the risks that arise while the agreement is being performed, fixes liability, governs payment, and sets out in advance how a disagreement will be resolved.

In practice, contract disputes do not only arise because one party has breached. They arise because the terms were imprecise from the outset, because payment and liability were left incompletely regulated, or because the text of the agreement did not reflect how the business actually operates.

The following seven points are worth settling before a business contract is signed.

## 1. Identify the parties and confirm signing authority

Record the registered name, state registration details and address of each legal entity, and establish the authority of the individual signing on its behalf.

Where someone other than the executive director signs, check whether that person holds the power to bind the company, and whether a power of attorney or other authorising document exists.

This matters most on high-value, long-term or otherwise significant agreements. Signing without verifying authority creates a risk of later dispute over whether the contract is valid at all, and over whether the obligations under it are enforceable.

## 2. Define the obligations precisely

Describing an obligation as "will provide services", "will supply goods" or "will cooperate" is not sufficient.

Set out which goods are to be supplied, what work is to be performed, the scope of the service, quality requirements, quantities, timing, the conditions for handover, and how performance is to be evidenced — in as much detail as the transaction allows.

Article 195.3 of the Civil Code treats as essential terms those required by law for the particular contract, those indispensable to it, and any term on which one party insists.

Where obligations are left vague, it becomes difficult to establish whether the work or service was performed properly, and which party is in breach.

## 3. Regulate payment in full

Stating the total price does not amount to a complete payment clause.

Specify whether payment is made in advance or on completion; the payment schedule where the price is paid in instalments; the payment deadline; whether VAT and other taxes are included; and which documents must be in place before the obligation to pay arises.

The contract can determine in advance which document triggers payment — a handover certificate, an invoice, or an electronic payment record.

It is also worth addressing whether undisputed amounts continue to be paid while a dispute over part of the performance is running. That single provision reduces the risk of the entire payment flow being suspended.

## 4. Set liability in line with the statutory limits

The consequences of breach should be stated expressly. A liability clause drafted in broad terms at an inflated figure does not, in practice, provide protection.

Article 232.4 of the Civil Code caps the aggregate penalty at 50 per cent of the value of the unperformed obligation, and article 232.6 limits the penalty for delay to 0.5 per cent of that value per day. Where the contract makes no provision for a penalty on late performance, no penalty can be claimed — but the right to claim the loss actually suffered remains.

Rather than tying liability mechanically to the total contract price, calibrate it to the type of obligation breached, the duration of the breach and the consequences that may follow from it.

Alongside a contractual penalty, it may be necessary to regulate recovery of loss separately. Under article 229.1 of the Civil Code, loss covers both actual damage to property and income that ought to have been earned.

## 5. State clearly how the contract ends

Set out the term, any extension, and the grounds for early termination.

Address whether a breach entitles the other party to terminate immediately or whether a period must first be given to remedy it; how much notice is required; and in what form that notice must be delivered.

Do not leave out the closing position: how completed work, payments, property, documents and information are accounted for once the contract has ended.

Articles 204 and 205 of the Civil Code govern withdrawal from a contract and its consequences, so a termination clause should be drafted to sit consistently with the type of contract and with those provisions.

## 6. Regulate electronic communication and notices

Contract drafts are exchanged, orders confirmed, work accepted and invoices delivered by email, through software and across electronic systems as a matter of routine.

The contract should therefore name each party's official email address, state whether notice sent to that address is treated as validly given, and set out how a change of address is to be notified.

Article 196.1.8 of the Civil Code recognises contracting in electronic form: the parties may express and mutually accept their intention using technical means and software.

Where an electronic form is used, it is important to retain records of who accepted which terms and when, in a form capable of being proved later.

## 7. Agree the dispute resolution procedure in advance

The contract should state which forum will determine a dispute, and under what procedure.

For domestic contracts, the choice between the courts and arbitration depends on the nature of the agreement and what the parties need from the process.

Where a foreign entity is a party, the forum is only part of the question. Governing law, the seat of the arbitration and the language of the proceedings all need to be agreed at the outset.

Article 8 of the Law on Arbitration allows the parties to agree to refer to arbitration disputes that have arisen or may arise out of a defined legal relationship, and requires that agreement to be in writing. The arbitration agreement may take the form of a clause within the main contract or of a separate agreement.

If arbitration is chosen, a general statement that "disputes shall be resolved by arbitration" is not enough. Name the institution and specify the rules that will apply.

## Conclusion

A business contract needs its provisions on parties and authority, core obligations, payment, liability, termination, electronic notice and dispute resolution to work together rather than in isolation.

The more precisely those terms are drafted, the lower the risk that the parties' understandings diverge during performance, that payment stalls, that performance becomes difficult to prove, or that liability is contested.

Reviewing a draft is not only a question of whether it complies with the law. It is also a question of whether each provision can be implemented in the business as it actually runs, what evidence will demonstrate performance, and what remedies are realistically available if something goes wrong.
