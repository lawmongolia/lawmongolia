# The true cost of a loan includes more than the advertised interest rate

Lending products are often promoted by placing the monthly interest rate prominently on the screen, while processing fees and other charges become apparent only at the final stage of the application. That approach creates a disclosure risk, particularly when an applicant is expected to compare and accept the terms of an app-based loan within minutes. The relevant comparison is not limited to the stated interest rate. Borrowers also need information about the cost of obtaining and maintaining the facility.

Mongolia's principal regulatory reference is the **Methodology for Calculating Deposit and Loan Interest Rates and Ensuring Information Transparency**, jointly approved by the Governor of the Bank of Mongolia and the Chair of the Financial Regulatory Commission (FRC) under Order A-106/298 of 23 May 2023, as amended by joint Order A/150, 370 of 20 May 2026. Section 3.1 requires banks and licensed non-bank financial institutions (NBFIs) to publish accurate, product-specific information on stated interest, the effective cost of borrowing, fees and charges on their websites. The methodology requires interest to be expressed annually, while section 3.3 prescribes an annualised effective-cost percentage rounded to two decimal places.

## The advertised interest rate and effective borrowing cost are different measures

The advertised rate expresses the price of using the principal under the loan's interest terms. Effective borrowing cost, by contrast, is a broader measure calculated under the methodology to capture specified costs borne in connection with the loan. Two facilities with identical principal, tenor and nominal interest can have different economic costs if one involves mandatory origination, monitoring, insurance or third-party charges.

For example, marketing a product as carrying “zero interest” does not establish that financing is cost-free where an applicant must also pay a compulsory processing fee. This does not mean that all charges are prohibited or that every expense associated with a transaction must be included in the effective-cost calculation. The nature and conditions of the charge, its connection with obtaining the loan, and the methodology's specific scope have to be assessed separately.

The distinction has operational significance for digital lenders. A product team may view an identity-verification charge as a technology cost, while the borrower experiences it as an unavoidable price of accessing credit. The lender's legal and finance teams must determine its appropriate treatment rather than relying solely on its accounting label.

## Payments to third parties may form part of the calculation

Section 3.4 of the methodology addresses specified fees and charges payable by the applicant during the loan-review process and within the relevant period after origination. It refers to application, assessment, disbursement and post-disbursement monitoring charges payable to the lender; insurance costs required by the lender, subject to the stated mortgage-related exception; and service fees payable to third parties in connection with the provision of the loan.

Section 3.4.4 also addresses commissions paid by a supplier to a lender in specified three-party motor-vehicle finance lease and fiduciary structures. Accordingly, the fact that a supplier rather than the borrower transfers a commission does not automatically remove the transaction from the methodology's analysis. The exact contractual structure and the statutory category of the charge matter.

A fintech loan may involve an application operator, a licensed lender, an insurer, an identity-verification provider and a merchant. They may each charge a different entity. Whether those charges fall within the effective-cost disclosure requirements cannot be decided simply by placing them in separate contracts or having another group company collect them. The economic function of the payment and the applicable regulatory wording must be considered together.

## Disclosure must be consistent throughout the digital application

Publishing a tariff document at the bottom of a website may not, by itself, demonstrate that an applicant understood the financial conditions presented during the application. Section 3.1 requires publicly available, product-specific disclosure. Section 3.5 further addresses a change in the previously disclosed effective cost after the credit decision: the reason must be explained, and the facility may proceed on the applicant's acceptance.

A sound digital implementation therefore records the terms shown at application, the price applicable when credit is approved, and the conditions accepted before the agreement is concluded. A concise comparison screen can present principal, duration, annual advertised interest, calculated effective borrowing cost, total repayment, instalment schedule and compulsory fees. This is a practical implementation of transparency, not a claim that the methodology mandates one particular user-interface design.

The distinction between conditions is especially important where loan offers are dynamic. If a customer receives an initial quote at one rate but the final loan approval includes a higher charge, reliance on an earlier generic click-through acceptance can make the applicable agreement difficult to prove. The contract document, current product tariff, customer interface and transaction log should be aligned.

## Care is required when applying the 2026 amendments

The amendments adopted on 20 May 2026 introduced provisions on benchmark reference rates for banks and changes to related disclosures. In particular, section 3.2, which requires information to be updated within one business day after specified changes, expressly refers to **banks**. It should not be represented as imposing the same one-business-day deadline automatically on every NBFI. The product-specific transparency obligation under section 3.1, however, remains material to licensed NBFIs.

Nor should product designers assume that effective-cost disclosure is only a marketing matter. The financial calculation needs to reconcile with payment flows, the identity of fee recipients, the contractual allocation of charges and the evidence of customer acceptance. Where separate companies participate in a loan's distribution, those issues often arise before the legal agreement is signed.

## Conclusion

The true cost of credit is a product-structuring and contractual issue as much as a financial formula. A lender should map the fees associated with each product, determine their regulatory treatment, calculate the applicable effective-cost measure and ensure that advertising, interface displays, contractual terms and operational records convey a consistent account. The earlier that work is carried out, the easier it is to prevent avoidable consumer disputes and regulatory discrepancies.
