Anyone familiar with American courtroom films recognises the classic image of a jury: a group of citizens deliberates separately and returns a verdict on whether the accused is guilty.

Did Mongolia ever have such a system?

Not exactly.

Mongolia did, however, develop a strong tradition of citizen participation in adjudication. For much of the twentieth century, ordinary citizens sat alongside professional judges and, at certain stages of that history, participated directly in judicial decision-making.

The origins of that model can be traced back almost a century.

## 1926: one judicial official and two people's representatives

The Rules on the Establishment of Courts adopted on 16 April 1926 provided that a khoshuun people's court would determine cases with one judicial official and two representatives elected by the people.

Historical research published through Mongolia's judicial research institutions identifies this provision as an early legal foundation for direct citizen participation in adjudication.

The numerical structure is striking: one professional adjudicator sat with two non-professional representatives. Citizen representatives outnumbered the professional judicial member.

## Ulaanbaatar had twelve representatives by 1927

The arrangement was not merely theoretical.

Historical records of the Ulaanbaatar court show that, in December 1926, local authorities selected the judicial official who would serve in the new city court together with 12 people's representatives and six substitute representatives. The Supreme Court's historical archive preserves the names of those representatives.

The early court system therefore reflected an explicit idea that adjudication should not be conducted exclusively by a small professional class.

## In 1953, hundreds of citizen representatives were elected

The institution became increasingly formalised over the following decades.

According to the Supreme Court's historical records, a decree dated 24 August 1953 provided for elections to people's courts. On 18 October 1953, those elections were conducted on the basis of universal, direct, equal and secret voting.

The result was the election of 76 people's judges and 716 people's representatives. Hundreds of ordinary citizens therefore held a formal role in the adjudicative system.

## Was this actually a jury

There is an important distinction.

In the classic Anglo-American jury model, jurors generally deliberate separately from the professional judge and deliver a verdict on questions of fact or guilt.

Mongolia's historical model was closer to what comparative law describes as a lay-judge system: non-professional citizens sitting with a professional judge in deciding the case.

This distinction became relevant in Mongolia's own constitutional litigation concerning citizen representatives. Materials reproduced in the Constitutional Court proceedings record that, before the 1990s, cases were commonly determined by a panel consisting of one judge and two people's representatives, with the representatives exercising adjudicative authority comparable to that of the professional judge.

The historical Mongolian model was therefore not a conventional American-style jury. It was closer to a mixed professional-and-lay judicial panel.

## The 1992 Constitution retained citizen participation

The democratic Constitution fundamentally changed Mongolia's judicial system, but it did not eliminate the principle of public participation in adjudication.

Article 52.2 of the Constitution continues to provide that, where a first-instance court determines a case collectively, representatives of citizens participate in accordance with procedures established by law.

The institutional form changed, but the underlying concept survived.

## The constitutional dispute over whether citizens could sit on the bench

The precise role of the citizen representative became a major constitutional issue in the 2010s.

Legislation enacted in 2012 contemplated citizen representatives participating as part of the court panel in certain proceedings.

In its Conclusion No. 04 of 13 November 2013, the Constitutional Court found that provisions placing citizen representatives within the judicial panel conflicted with article 49.2 of the Constitution, which prohibits interference with judges in the exercise of judicial functions.

Parliament did not accept that conclusion. The matter therefore proceeded to the Constitutional Court sitting in its full composition. In Decision No. 01 dated 8 January 2014, the Court finally invalidated the relevant provisions concerning citizen representatives sitting as members of the judicial panel.

That decision marks a key dividing line between the historic people's-representative model and the modern institution.

## What does a citizen representative do today

Under article 10.1 of the current Law on Courts, representatives of citizens participate in first-instance proceedings for the purposes of transparency, justice and public oversight.

They do not cast a judicial vote equivalent to that of a judge.

Article 66 instead allows citizen representatives to participate in the examination of evidence, ask questions, and express a written or oral conclusion concerning the facts of the case and the guilt or responsibility of the relevant party. The judicial panel must consider that conclusion and explain its treatment of it in the judgment.

The modern citizen representative is therefore neither a juror in the classic sense nor a judge. The institution is a distinct Mongolian mechanism for introducing citizen participation and public perspective into formal adjudication.

## Nearly a century of one recurring question

In 1926, one professional judicial official and two elected representatives could determine a case together.

In 1953, 716 people's representatives were elected.

The 1992 Constitution preserved citizen participation.

In 2014, the Constitutional Court drew a constitutional boundary preventing citizen representatives from becoming members of the judicial bench.

Today, citizen representatives participate in evidentiary examination and provide conclusions without themselves deciding the case.

The institutional arrangements are very different. But they all revolve around the same enduring question: should justice be administered exclusively by professional judges, or should ordinary citizens also have a structured role in the judicial process?

Mongolian legal history has been answering that question in different ways for almost one hundred years.
