On 27 March 1926, Mongolia did not yet have its modern court hierarchy. The Supreme Court had not been created, and the legal framework for a nationwide unified court system would follow only several weeks later.

Yet according to historical materials published by Mongolia's Judicial General Council, that day saw what is recorded as Mongolia's first public court hearing. The case concerned 19-year-old Soviet citizen Barmatov Inketiy Nikolaevich and others who had crossed into Mongolian territory and became involved in an armed confrontation with border forces.

The proceedings were conducted by the Temporary Court attached to the Government. Historical records identify Kh. Choibalsan as chairing the court, with S. Delgersan and N. Nasanbat participating as members.

What makes the case significant is not its criminal facts, but the institutional moment in which it occurred.

## A public trial before the unified court system

The Barmatov hearing took place on 27 March 1926. Mongolia's Rules on the Establishment of Courts, which provided the legal foundation for a unified system of courts, were adopted only on 16 April 1926.

Those rules initiated the establishment of courts across five aimags, three cities and 71 khoshuuns.

The Supreme Court came later still. A government meeting on 24 December 1926 resolved to establish the Supreme Court and commence its operation from 1 January 1927. Its first sitting was held on 3 January 1927, and it received its official seal the following day.

Mongolia's first recorded public trial therefore predates both the formal nationwide court framework and the Supreme Court itself.

## Public proceedings, evidence and defence

The Temporary Court should not be equated with a modern independent judiciary. Nevertheless, historical accounts describe several procedural elements that are recognisable today.

A published historical account of the Temporary Court records that proceedings could be conducted collectively and in public, that evidence was to form the basis of adjudication, and that a person defending the accused could participate, review case materials and meet privately with the defendant.

For Mongolia in the 1920s, this represented an important institutional transition. Adjudication was beginning to emerge as a distinct public process rather than an internal function of administrative government.

## But the court was not independent in the modern constitutional sense

The same historical material reveals a very different feature of the system.

Decisions of the Temporary Court were subject to approval by the Government and were not appealable in the modern sense.

A historical publication reproducing the ruling in the Barmatov case states that Barmatov was sentenced to death and four co-defendants to terms of imprisonment. It further records that the relevant government body approved the ruling on 13 April 1926 and that the security authority reported implementation of the decision on 17 April.

Such a structure is fundamentally different from Mongolia's current constitutional model. Article 49 of the Constitution provides that judges are independent and subject only to law, and prohibits public officials and other persons from interfering with adjudication. Article 5 of the current Law on Courts likewise requires judicial power to be exercised independently of any person or institution.

## Why the public nature of the trial mattered

Judicial historical materials describe the March 1926 hearing as an extraordinary event for the public at the time.

Its significance extended beyond the fate of particular defendants. It provided a visible demonstration that questions of criminal responsibility could be considered through a defined proceeding in which evidence was examined before the public.

Today the principle of open justice may appear ordinary. In early twentieth-century Mongolia, public adjudication was emerging at the same time as the judicial institution itself.

## A historical paradox

The case captures an important paradox in Mongolia's judicial history.

On one side were emerging procedural ideas: public hearings, evidentiary adjudication and access to a defence.

On the other, the final judicial decision remained linked to executive approval, and there was no ordinary system of appellate review.

The development of Mongolia's judiciary was therefore not a single transition from an old system to a modern one. Open justice, defence rights, appellate review and judicial independence emerged at different times and through different institutional reforms.

## Conclusion

The Barmatov case is not significant because the 1926 proceeding can be measured against modern criminal procedure and found equivalent.

Its significance lies in showing that public adjudication emerged in Mongolia before judicial independence had fully developed.

Within weeks of the hearing, the legal foundation of a nationwide court system was adopted. Within months, the Supreme Court began operating.

Behind Mongolia's current judicial system lies not one reform, but almost a century of institutional development.
