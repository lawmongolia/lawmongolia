Disputes between shareholders can bring a company to an effective standstill. A common pattern in Mongolian small and medium-sized companies is a genuine deadlock between two equal shareholders: neither side can pass a resolution, and meetings cannot produce a valid decision.

Mongolian company law provides a route for seeking dissolution of a company in this situation. Before pursuing it, however, several legal and practical questions need to be carefully assessed.

## Not every shareholder can apply for dissolution

Article 90.1.5 of the Company Law provides a specific ground for dissolving a company. It applies where a shareholder or shareholders holding a majority of the company's total shares apply to court on the basis that the company can no longer continue its operations.

Three conditions apply together. First, the applicant must hold a majority of the company's total shares. Second, it must be established that the company can no longer continue operating. Third, the application must be made to court.

Two equal 50 per cent shareholders will generally not, individually, satisfy the majority-shareholding requirement. Where the two shareholders apply jointly, or where one genuinely holds a majority, the position is different.

## A disagreement alone does not establish that the company cannot continue

In practice, the more difficult question is how to prove that the company can no longer continue operating.

A disagreement between two shareholders preventing meetings from being held may support that conclusion, but whether it is sufficient on its own depends on the facts of the case as assessed by the court.

A general assertion that "we disagree" is not enough. Evidence is generally needed of concrete attempts to convene a board or shareholders' meeting, failed votes, disruption to the company's core operations, and an inability to make financial decisions, supported by dates and figures.

## Dissolution is a last resort, not the only outcome

Before ordering dissolution, the court may consider an alternative remedy.

Article 90.2 allows the court to grant a request by the company or other shareholders to purchase the shares of the shareholder seeking dissolution, instead of ordering dissolution.

This matters considerably in practice. Dissolution is an irreversible measure — employees are terminated, contracts with counterparties end, and licences may be lost. A share buy-out, by contrast, preserves the company as a going concern and resolves only the ownership dispute.

Both the party applying for dissolution and the respondent should consider this alternative early. In practice, many such disputes are ultimately resolved not by dissolution, but by one party buying out the other's shares at an agreed price.

## Valuing the shares is itself a major issue

Choosing the buy-out route creates a further question: who determines the share price, and how.

Where the company's charter or a shareholders' agreement does not already provide a valuation mechanism, the parties may need to appoint an independent valuer or apply to the court for a valuation, taking into account the company's financial statements, asset value and earning capacity.

For this reason, setting out a clear buy-out mechanism and valuation methodology in the company's constitutional documents in advance is one of the most effective protections against a future deadlock dispute.

## What to check in practice

Where a dispute arises between two equal shareholders, the first step should not be litigation. It should be a review of the company's own constitutional documents: the procedure for convening meetings, the threshold for board decisions, any right of first refusal on a share sale, and the dispute resolution mechanism set out in the charter or shareholders' agreement.

Where no such mechanism exists, exploring a negotiated buy-out before resorting to court may be considerably more efficient in time and cost.

## Conclusion

Mongolian law provides a route to seek dissolution where shareholders reach deadlock, but it is not an automatic remedy. The level of shareholding, the sufficiency of the evidence, and the availability of a buy-out as an alternative to dissolution all need to be assessed together.

Addressing this risk in the shareholders' agreement at the time a company is formed is a far cheaper and faster solution than litigating it later.
