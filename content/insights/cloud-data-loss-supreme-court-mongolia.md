Cloud infrastructure, SaaS platforms and outsourced data storage have become standard components of business operations. A more difficult legal question arises when data stored on a third-party server is lost: who bears the risk, and can the provider avoid liability simply because the customer did not purchase an additional backup service?

The Supreme Court of Mongolia addressed these issues in Decision No. 001/ХТ2025/00208 dated 18 November 2025, with a case summary published in 2026.

## How the dispute arose

The parties had entered electronically into a cloud services agreement. The customer hosted software and business data on a virtual server operated on the provider's cloud infrastructure. The data was subsequently lost and the customer sought compensation for the costs associated with restoring and reconstructing the information.

The court of first instance dismissed the claim; the appellate court awarded part of the loss. The Supreme Court upheld the outcome but classified the contract differently.

## A cloud contract need not be a lease or a services contract

The court of first instance treated the relationship as a lease, and the appellate court as a contract for work.

The Supreme Court found that the agreement contained characteristics of lease, storage, services and sale, but that no single statutory contract type predominated. It therefore treated the arrangement as an innominate contract under article 189.5 of the Civil Code, with the general rules on contractual obligations applying.

This matters for technology contracting generally: SaaS, API, cloud and platform agreements do not have to be forced into one of the traditional contract types set out in the Civil Code.

## The absence of a backup service does not automatically release the provider

The most interesting part of the dispute concerned backup.

The provider's terms placed certain risks on a customer that had not purchased an additional backup service. The Supreme Court nonetheless held that the provider had a core obligation to ensure the reliable operation of the cloud infrastructure and the customer's uninterrupted use of the virtual server.

Loss of the data prevented the customer from receiving the contracted service and constituted a breach of that core obligation. In the circumstances of this case, the absence of an additional backup service did not transfer that responsibility to the customer.

The judgment should not be read as imposing unlimited liability on every cloud provider. The courts awarded only MNT 41,440,568 of the losses that were sufficiently established by evidence and rejected the remainder.

## What businesses should take from this

In a cloud, SaaS or data storage agreement, a single general sentence stating that "the customer is responsible for information security" is not enough. The obligations of each party need to be separated out.

At a minimum: the provider's infrastructure and server availability; the customer's own software and configuration; who is responsible for backup; backup frequency; recovery time; the steps to be taken when the system fails; and the retention of logs and incident reports.

The case also shows that proving that data was lost is not enough to recover a loss. The actual cost of restoring the information, payments to third parties, and additional staff and service costs all have to be evidenced with financial records.

## Conclusion

In technology contracts, the label attached to the agreement matters less than the actual allocation of obligations and risk. Where the provider's core obligations and the customer's backup and security duties are not clearly defined, a standard liability exclusion will not resolve the question on its own.
