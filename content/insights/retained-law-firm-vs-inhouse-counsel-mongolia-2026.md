When a company looks for a **retained law firm in Mongolia**, the starting question should not be the monthly price. It should be which business decisions require regular legal involvement. Supplier contracts, employment documentation, collections, customer data, licences and regulatory questions arise throughout the year, but the right arrangement varies by organisation.

A **retained legal services** engagement typically means instructing an external legal advisory firm to provide continuing assistance for an agreed scope and period. It should not be confused automatically with engaging a licensed advocate for court representation. Articles 3, 18, 19 and 22 of Mongolia's [Law on Advocacy](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/14859) are relevant to the distinction between legal advisory work, the professional organisation of advocacy, and advocacy engagements.

## Model one: an in-house lawyer

An in-house lawyer can work directly with management, finance, human resources and commercial teams. Familiarity with the company's operations and decision-making processes is a substantial benefit. The limitation is breadth: one employee may not be equipped to handle investment transactions, complex fintech licensing, software intellectual property and litigation at the same time.

An employer should define the legal team's authority, escalation process and the matters for which external specialist counsel will be instructed.

## Model two: an external retained law firm

A **retained law firm** can review contracts, prepare legal opinions, support board and shareholder documentation, advise on policies and regulatory changes, and assist with pre-dispute strategy. This model may suit a business without a dedicated legal department or one that needs defined external expertise.

The term “ongoing counsel” does not mean unlimited service. A practical contract should state the categories of work, authorised contacts, ordinary and urgent response times, meeting arrangements, workload limits and approval of additional fees.

## Model three: in-house counsel and an external firm together

A hybrid arrangement can be valuable to technology companies, financial service providers and organisations with multiple business lines. In-house counsel coordinates instructions and implementation while the external firm undertakes specialist opinions, major transactions, sensitive disputes or an independent review of a proposed course of action.

This requires an agreed lead on each matter. Otherwise, instructions may be duplicated and deadlines may be missed while responsibility is assumed to sit elsewhere.

## Six questions to settle before signing a retainer

1. **Scope:** which contracts, corporate documents, employment issues and compliance matters are covered?
2. **Service levels:** what response time applies to urgent advice, routine requests and complex reviews?
3. **Ownership:** who may instruct the firm and who is the responsible lawyer?
4. **Fees:** what is included, what requires additional approval and how are expenses treated?
5. **Confidentiality and conflicts:** how are sensitive records handled and potential opposing interests identified?
6. **Litigation:** are pre-action demands, claim drafting, hearings and appeals included or separately instructed?

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a retained law firm replace an in-house legal department?** Not necessarily. It may replace, supplement or provide specialist support to the internal function.

**Is there a statutory monthly price for retained advice?** A universal market price should not be assumed. Scope, capacity, service levels and the engagement terms determine the commercial proposal.

**Does a corporate retainer include litigation?** Only where expressly agreed and handled with the necessary professional and procedural authority. Read more about [business litigation representation](/insights/business-litigation-attorney-contract-disputes-mongolia-2026).

**Conclusion:** A company selecting retained legal counsel should compare the legal risks it faces, the real service scope, responsiveness and escalation into regulated advocacy rather than relying on the lowest quoted monthly fee.
