An individual or company that lends money and does not receive repayment on time may have claims for principal, contractual interest and other sums lawfully due. Those claims must be established separately. A bank transfer may prove that money was paid, but it does not necessarily prove that the transaction was a loan, that an interest rate was agreed or that a late-payment penalty applies. The relevant documents, agreed repayment date, payments already made and statutory restrictions determine the amount that can be claimed.

Articles 281–283 of Mongolia's [Civil Code](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail?lawId=299) govern loan agreements. Additional rules may apply to bank and non-bank financial institution lending and to persons regularly carrying on money-lending activities for profit. Different lending relationships should not be treated as legally identical.

## Proving that a loan was made

Under Article 281.1, the lender provides money or other fungible property and the borrower undertakes to return the corresponding amount or property as agreed. Relevant evidence includes a written loan agreement, bank transfer records, a cash receipt, a repayment schedule, correspondence and records of partial repayment.

A dispute can arise where a transfer description does not identify the payment as a loan or money was paid to a third-party account. In such a case, the purpose of the transaction may need to be demonstrated through surrounding correspondence, acknowledgement of the debt, earlier repayment behaviour and reconciled balances. There is a difference between establishing that money was transferred and establishing the legal obligation to return it as a loan.

Interest requires separate consideration. Under Article 282.3, where interest is agreed the loan contract must ordinarily be in writing, unless another law provides otherwise. Failure to satisfy the applicable form requirement can result in loss of the right to contractual interest even where the claim to repayment of principal remains available.

## Ordinary interest, default interest and penalties

Ordinary loan interest is the agreed consideration for the use of the money. Article 282.1 addresses agreed interest in a one-off or non-profit lending arrangement, and Article 282.2 permits a court to reduce manifestly prejudicial interest on the borrower's application in the circumstances provided by law.

A contractual late-payment penalty is a different legal claim. Article 232.3 ordinarily requires the penalty arrangement to be made in writing. Article 232.4 limits the aggregate contractual forfeiture to **50 percent of the value of the unperformed obligation**, while Article 232.6 sets a maximum of **0.5 percent per day** for a daily late-payment penalty. A penalty is not automatically payable merely because the loan is overdue. The contract or applicable law must provide a proper basis for it.

For example, assume that MNT 10,000,000 of principal remains unpaid and a valid contractual term provides for a 0.2 percent daily penalty on that outstanding sum. Thirty days of delay would produce an illustrative penalty of MNT 600,000. The calculation assumes that the entire amount remained unpaid throughout that period. It must also remain within the statutory total cap and comply with any other applicable provisions.

Loans issued by authorised financial institutions may involve ordinary interest, additional interest following default and other contractually or statutorily permitted charges. Articles 451–453 of the Civil Code and the relevant sectoral legislation require separate consideration. The labels and amounts should be reconciled to avoid unsupported duplication of claims.

## Special rules for regular money-lending businesses

The [Law on the Regulation of Money-Lending Activities](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail?lawId=16532149634331) governs specified pawn-lending and regular for-profit money-lending activities by individuals. It is not a general rule for every occasional loan between relatives or commercial counterparties.

A [resolution of the Money-Lending Activities Policy Council dated 26 February 2025](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail?lawId=17430649653802) fixed the maximum monthly interest rate for lenders within that regulatory regime at **4.5 percent**. This figure must not be presented as a universal ceiling automatically applicable to banks, non-bank financial institutions and all one-off private loans. The correct category of lender and loan must first be identified.

## What happens if the agreement contains no repayment date?

Article 283.1 of the Civil Code provides that, where no repayment date was agreed, the borrower must repay within one month after the lender makes the repayment demand. The date and evidence of receipt of that demand can therefore be legally significant.

A written demand can set out the principal, relevant interest, contractual penalties, partial payments already made, the amount outstanding and the date by which payment is requested. A mutually confirmed statement of account may help clarify the amount in dispute, although the legal consequences of an acknowledgement must be evaluated from the particular document.

## Limitation periods and claims in court

Article 75.2.1 of the Civil Code generally establishes a **three-year limitation period for claims arising from contractual obligations**, subject to special rules. The clock does not necessarily run from the date the money was transferred. It is necessary to identify when the repayment claim became enforceable, whether several instalments have different due dates and whether events affecting limitation have occurred.

Partial payment, acknowledgement of an obligation or filing a claim may affect limitation under Articles 76–79, depending on the circumstances. A lender approaching a possible time bar should not rely solely on repeated informal telephone requests.

If the borrower does not perform, the lender may commence civil proceedings, pleading principal, legally recoverable interest, penalties and any other available remedies on their separate factual and legal bases. The supporting material will generally include the agreement, proof of disbursement, calculations, repayments, demands and relevant communications.

Obtaining a judgment is distinct from actually recovering the funds. A final enforceable judgment may still require formal enforcement, and the outcome will depend on the debtor's available assets, lawful enforcement measures, collateral and competing rights.

Before litigation, the practical assessment should address five matters together: whether a loan and transfer of funds can be proven, what principal remains due, whether interest and penalties are legally supported, whether the claim is within the applicable limitation period and whether a judgment is likely to be enforceable in practice.
