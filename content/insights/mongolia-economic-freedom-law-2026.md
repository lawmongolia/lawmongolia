On 3 July 2026, the Parliament of Mongolia adopted the Law on Economic Freedom, a framework statute intended to protect property rights, guarantee freedom to conduct business, promote fair competition and improve the stability of the business environment. The law and a broad package of related amendments were published in Issue No. 31 of the State Information bulletin on 21 August 2026.

The law does not replace sector-specific regulation governing licensing, taxation, employment, financial services or competition. Its significance lies instead in setting horizontal standards that public authorities must observe when regulating businesses.

## Regulation must be proportionate and predictable

Article 3 establishes that businesses should be free to conduct activities that are not prohibited by law. Restrictions on business activity must be appropriate to their purpose and kept at the minimum possible level. Regulation must also be stable and predictable, lawfully acquired property rights must be protected, and businesses must remain free to determine contractual terms within the limits of law.

This does not eliminate legitimate regulation for national security, fundamental rights, public health, environmental protection or fair competition. It does, however, provide a statutory basis for questioning whether a particular regulatory measure is lawful, proportionate and necessary.

## Six-month lead time for cost-increasing legislation

One of the most commercially significant provisions is article 4.4. Except for the annual budget law and legislation prepared in connection with it, legislation that would negatively affect business profitability or increase business costs must be scheduled to take effect no earlier than six months after its adoption.

The provision effectively introduces a regulatory lead-time principle. Businesses facing new compliance systems, equipment requirements, reporting obligations or operational standards should therefore examine not only what the new rule requires, but also whether its commencement date complies with article 4.4.

The exception for annual budget legislation matters. The six-month rule should not be treated as automatically applicable to every fiscal or regulatory measure without first examining the legal instrument concerned.

## Authorities cannot invent additional document requirements

Article 7.1.7 prohibits public authorities from requiring documents or application materials that are not prescribed by law or an administrative normative act. Article 7.1.8 similarly prohibits inspections that are not authorised by law and inspections extending beyond the legally prescribed period.

The law also prohibits compulsory participation of businesses in corporate social responsibility activities, unjustified preferential treatment of selected businesses, improper interference with business operations, and unlawful seizure of business property.

These rules are potentially significant in day-to-day dealings with licensing, registration and inspection authorities. A request justified only by internal practice should no longer be accepted without identifying the statutory or normative basis for it.

## The once-only principle is moving into implementation

Article 3.1.12 requires public services to be digital, transparent, prompt and equally accessible, and establishes the principle that the same information should be obtained from a business only once.

The Government's implementation plan, approved by Resolution No. 329 dated 5 August 2026, includes converting inter-agency certificates used in digital services into API-based exchanges, improving the E-Business platform and moving 80 per cent of government information exchange into digital form in 2027.

On 23 September 2026, the Government also announced measures requiring documents to be collected only once throughout the permit application and issuance process, and approved rules for the integrated permit and notification information system.

## Remedies against unlawful administrative action

Article 5.1 expressly recognises a business's right to complain against unlawful government decisions or actions. The relevant authority must resolve the complaint within the statutory period, restore violated rights and compensate loss in accordance with applicable law.

This does not create an automatic damages mechanism. Jurisdiction, causation, evidence and the recovery procedure remain subject to the relevant administrative and civil-law framework. The new law nevertheless provides a clearer statutory foundation for challenging regulatory action that unlawfully interferes with business rights.

## Practical implications

Businesses should increasingly document their regulatory interactions. Where an authority requires additional materials, the business should identify the precise legal basis. Where an unlawful requirement causes delay or additional cost, evidence of that impact should be preserved. New regulations that increase compliance costs should be reviewed against the six-month lead-time rule, and unlawful administrative decisions should be challenged within the applicable statutory deadlines.

## Conclusion

The Law on Economic Freedom does not itself eliminate administrative burdens. Its practical effect will depend on the alignment of sector legislation and on how consistently public authorities apply its principles.

It nevertheless changes the analytical starting point. A regulatory requirement should no longer be assessed merely on the basis that an authority has imposed it. Businesses now have a clearer statutory basis to ask whether a requirement is legally authorised, proportionate, transparent and no more restrictive than necessary.
