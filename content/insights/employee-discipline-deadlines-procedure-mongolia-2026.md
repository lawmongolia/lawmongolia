When an employee fails to perform assigned duties or breaches workplace rules, how long does an employer have to impose a disciplinary measure? May the employer issue an order immediately, or must the employee first be offered an opportunity to explain? [Articles 122 and 123 of Mongolia's Labour Law](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail?lawId=16230709635751) answer these questions. The law authorizes disciplinary measures, but subjects that authority to **time limits, procedural safeguards and substantive restrictions**.

## What constitutes disciplinary misconduct?

**Article 123.1** defines disciplinary misconduct as an employee's culpable act or omission that breaches labour legislation, the employment contract, internal employment rules or the job description. Not every unfavorable workplace outcome is therefore disciplinary misconduct. An employer must identify the obligation concerned, establish whether the employee knew or should have known of it, and examine the employee's culpability.

For example, the mere fact that equipment purchased for an employee was not used may not be sufficient. The employer should determine whether use was required by the employment contract, job description, internal rules or a lawful instruction; whether the necessary conditions for use were provided; and whether the employee refused without justification. **Article 122.2** also requires termination grounds and disciplinary violations to be specified in internal employment rules, and newly adopted or amended rules to be brought to employees' attention.

## Which disciplinary measures are permitted?

**Article 123.2** provides five forms of discipline: a private warning delivered individually; a public warning announced to the workforce; reduction of basic salary by up to 20 percent for up to three months; demotion; and employer-initiated termination of employment. The employer should not create an additional monetary "fine" and treat it as though it were one of these statutory disciplinary measures.

The measure chosen must reflect the nature and consequences of the violation. Demotion can also raise separate questions concerning the employee's new position, duties and contractual terms. Authority to impose a disciplinary sanction is not an unlimited power to change all other terms of an employment contract without regard to the applicable legal rules.

## Six months from misconduct and one month from discovery

Under **Article 123.4**, an employer must impose the sanction **within six months** after the misconduct, or the last day of continuing misconduct, and **within one month** after the employer discovered it. These periods start from different events and must both be assessed. The employer should be able to establish the date of misconduct and the date of discovery with reliable records.

Suppose a one-off breach occurred on 1 February 2026 and the employer discovered it on 20 July 2026. Although less than one month may have passed after discovery, the separate six-month outer limit still matters. The employer cannot disregard the latter merely because the discovery period has not elapsed. This example illustrates the two starting points; it does not account for any statutory interruption of time or other special circumstances.

**Article 123.5** provides a **special one-year period measured from the misconduct** for employees subject to full property liability. This does not mean that all employees are governed by one year. The employer must examine whether the employee lawfully falls into that category and how the discovery-based limit applies in the specific circumstances.

## Events that interrupt the statutory periods

**Article 123.6** addresses interruption of the periods under Articles 123.4 and 123.5 while the employee has a medical certificate, takes annual leave or personal leave, or while the violation is investigated by a law-enforcement, audit or other competent authority. An employer relying on this provision should document the relevant dates and legal basis with medical certificates, leave orders or investigation records. Management's decision to delay a meeting, or an employee's mere failure to provide an explanation, should not automatically be treated as a statutory ground of interruption.

## Notice and an employee explanation are mandatory procedural safeguards

Under **Article 123.3**, the employer must notify the employee and obtain an explanation before imposing discipline. The employer must consider the nature and consequences of the misconduct when selecting a sanction. The disciplinary decision must be issued in writing or electronically. It is therefore insufficient to assume that an internal violation report alone automatically justifies an immediate disciplinary order. The employee must have a genuine opportunity to explain relevant circumstances and provide supporting evidence.

If an employee declines to explain, the employer should document when the request was delivered, what opportunity was provided, and how the refusal occurred. Such a record does not replace proof of the underlying misconduct. The breach, culpability and appropriateness of the sanction remain separate issues requiring substantiation.

## May two disciplinary sanctions be imposed for one violation?

**Article 123.7** prohibits multiple disciplinary sanctions for the same violation. Cutting basic salary and subsequently demoting an employee for precisely the same act may breach this rule. Disciplinary measures and a claim for proven damage to employer property may, however, arise on distinct legal grounds. Any property-liability claim must be considered separately under the Labour Law rather than automatically added to the disciplinary punishment.

Under **Article 123.8**, an employee is deemed free of the disciplinary sanction after one year has passed since it was imposed. **Article 123.9** permits the employer to lift the sanction earlier, with written or electronic notice to the employee. That one-year period concerns the continued status of an already imposed sanction; it must not be confused with the deadlines for initially imposing one.

## Where and when can an employee challenge discipline?

Under **Article 154.2.2**, labour-rights disputes other than those specified in Article 154.2.1 are generally submitted for preliminary dispute resolution **within 90 days** after the party knew or ought to have known that its rights were violated. **Article 154.2.1** sets a **30-day period from receipt of the employer's decision** for challenges to dismissal, termination, transfer or reassignment. Because a disciplinary demotion may in substance involve transfer to another position, the applicable period should be examined carefully; acting within 30 days is prudent where that classification is possible.

Ordinarily, the employee applies to the workplace labour-rights dispute resolution commission; where no such commission exists, the competent district or soum tripartite labour-rights dispute resolution committee is the relevant preliminary body. **Article 158** allows specified disputes to proceed directly to court. If a deadline was missed for a valid reason, **Article 157.1** provides for a court to determine whether it should be restored. Employees should preserve the disciplinary order, notices, explanation, employment contract, job description, internal rules and relevant electronic records.
