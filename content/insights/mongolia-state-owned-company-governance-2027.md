On 2 July 2026, the Parliament of Mongolia adopted the Law on Governance of State- and Locally-Owned Companies. The law was published in Issue No. 33 of the State Information bulletin on 4 September 2026 and will take effect on 1 January 2027.

The law is not merely an internal governance reform for public-sector entities. It has direct implications for private suppliers, contractors, lenders, investors and advisers dealing with state- and locally-owned companies.

## State-owned companies are expected to compete on market terms

Article 5 requires state- and locally-owned companies to operate transparently and efficiently, compete fairly in the market without unjustified privileges, refrain from abusing monopoly or dominant positions, maintain operational independence and remain free from political interference.

This is an important policy direction: a company should not obtain an unjustified commercial advantage merely because the state or a local government owns or controls it.

## More independent and professional boards

For commercial state- and locally-owned companies, the board must consist of five, seven or nine members. A five-member board must have at least two independent directors, a seven-member board at least three, and a nine-member board at least four. Executive management may not simultaneously serve as a board member.

The law also strengthens board-level compliance and risk oversight. Boards must address risks relating not only to finance, but to human rights, corruption, employment, information security, personal data, competition, environmental matters, taxation, and health and safety. Certain board committees must have at least two-thirds independent membership.

These board requirements will be introduced progressively rather than overnight. The transitional provisions allow the article 24 board requirements to be implemented in stages until 30 April 2028.

## Executive authority should be checked, not assumed

The board appoints executive management under a performance mandate agreement for a term of up to three years. The agreement must address objectives, financial and operational performance measures, remuneration, powers and responsibilities, grounds for suspension or removal, and reporting obligations.

For private counterparties, this makes authority verification increasingly important. The fact that a chief executive signs a contract should not automatically be treated as proof that no further corporate approval is required. Depending on the company's charter, the nature and value of the transaction and applicable company-law rules, board or shareholder approval may still be necessary.

For substantial supply, construction, financing, outsourcing and long-term service agreements, counterparties should confirm signatory authority and obtain evidence of any required corporate approvals as part of closing.

## Confidentiality clauses must account for statutory disclosure

Article 48 imposes extensive public disclosure obligations. State- and locally-owned companies must disclose information including budgets, business plans, five years of audited financial statements, risk-management information, and information on major and conflict-of-interest transactions.

Of particular importance to private counterparties, the law requires disclosure of information concerning procurements, suppliers, contract duration, contract value, the selection method and implementation progress.

A blanket contractual provision stating that all commercial terms are confidential therefore cannot override mandatory statutory disclosure. Contracts with state-owned companies should contain an express carve-out for disclosures required by law.

This does not mean that every piece of commercially sensitive information becomes public. The law recognises information protected under state secrecy, organisational secrecy and other restricted-information legislation. Businesses should identify and classify genuine trade secrets, technical information and protected personal data rather than relying on an undifferentiated confidentiality clause.

## Stronger audit and compliance will affect contract administration

The reform strengthens board oversight, audit functions, risk management and public accountability. Private contractors should expect transaction documentation to receive greater scrutiny.

Contract variations, acceptance certificates, pricing justifications, invoices, payment approvals and changes in scope should therefore be documented carefully. Informal management-level arrangements that are not reflected in valid corporate approvals or contractual documentation may become increasingly difficult to defend in an audit or compliance review.

## Reorganisation creates a separate counterparty risk

Article 50.1 requires existing state and locally owned business-accounting industrial entities established under the previous framework to be reorganised as companies or public-service institutions, or liquidated, within one year after the new law begins to apply.

Implementation has already started. On 9 September 2026, the Government approved measures identifying state-owned industrial entities for conversion into companies or public-service institutions and others for merger or liquidation. The Government reported that 25 entities were affected by those measures.

Private parties with long-term contracts should therefore check the legal-successor position of their public-sector counterparties. Key questions include who will assume receivables and liabilities, whether guarantees and security remain effective, whether bank and invoicing details will change, and whether an assignment, novation or contractual amendment will be required.

## Conclusion

The new governance law is broader than a board-composition reform. It changes how public companies compete, how directors and executives are appointed, how risks are governed, what commercial information must be disclosed and how existing state-owned industrial entities will be restructured.

For private counterparties, the practical preparation for 2027 is straightforward but important: re-check the legal status of the counterparty, signatory authority, required corporate approvals, statutory disclosure obligations and any pending reorganisation before entering into or extending material contracts.
