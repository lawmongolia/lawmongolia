An electronic transfer can be processed successfully by a bank even when the payer entered the wrong account number. Successful processing, however, does not mean that the unintended recipient automatically acquires the legal right to spend the funds as their own. A Mongolian criminal judgment delivered in May 2026 illustrates the distinction between receiving an erroneous transfer and knowingly misappropriating it.

## A rent payment reached the wrong bank account

On 23 February 2026, an individual attempted to transfer MNT 2 million as rent. The payer entered incorrect account details, and the funds were credited to a person other than the intended landlord. After discovering the error, the payer contacted the account holder and requested repayment. The money was not promptly returned, although the recipient subsequently made a partial repayment.

In Judgment **2026/ШЦТ/57 of 7 May 2026**, the Inter-Soum Court at Zamiin-Uud in Dornogovi Province found that the recipient had knowingly misappropriated funds credited through an erroneous payment. Applying Article 17.5(1) of Mongolia's Criminal Code, the court imposed a fine of MNT 650,000. The operative part also ordered the payment of MNT 1.1 million to the victim under the civil compensation provisions. This article concerns that first-instance judgment; the outcome of any subsequent appeal has not been verified.

The significance of the case is not merely that the original transfer was mistaken. The court assessed what the recipient knew, how the recipient responded after being informed of the mistake, and what documentary evidence supported the parties' respective accounts.

## Receipt of funds and criminal misappropriation are separate matters

[Article 17.5 of the Criminal Code](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail?lawId=11634) addresses, among other things, the knowing misappropriation of funds received through an erroneous payment where the prescribed statutory harm threshold is met. The law does not make every unintended incoming transfer a criminal offence. The critical questions concern knowledge, subsequent conduct, intent, the resulting harm and the evidence proving those elements.

For that reason, a recipient cannot safely argue that money became theirs merely because it appeared in their account. Equally, an unexpected credit that an account holder has not yet noticed should not automatically be equated with intentional criminal conduct. Criminal responsibility must be established by applying the statutory elements to the evidence of the particular case.

Civil law raises a related but distinct set of questions. The absence of a lawful basis for a payment may create a restitution claim even where criminal misappropriation cannot be established. Determining the appropriate defendant also matters. The analysis may differ where the money reached an ordinary current account, was credited against another person's loan or remained with a payment intermediary. An unsuccessful criminal complaint does not in itself determine whether a civil recovery claim exists.

## Why transaction records and messages matter

In the 2026 case, the evidence included interbank transfer records, bank statements, messages and witness or party testimony. These materials helped reconstruct the chronology: when the money was transferred, when the mistake was communicated and what the recipient did afterwards.

In practice, a payer may recall several telephone calls and repayment requests but have limited evidence of their contents. A traceable payment reference, screenshots of the transaction confirmation and preserved written communications provide stronger documentation. Digital evidence should also be kept in a form that allows its origin and integrity to be verified rather than relying exclusively on edited screenshots.

For banks and fintech operators, the case highlights the value of a documented erroneous-payment workflow. Institutions should retain relevant payment identifiers and records of complaints, evaluate requests under their contractual and regulatory obligations, and distinguish customer-assistance processes from powers to reverse transfers. A bank should not assume that it can freely move money from a recipient's account simply because another customer claims to have made a mistake; privacy, contractual rights and applicable payment rules remain relevant.

## Practical implications for both sides of an erroneous transfer

A payer should preserve transaction details, promptly notify the bank through an official channel and document any request for repayment. The fact that the payer made the error does not necessarily eliminate legal remedies, but it may affect the procedure and parties against whom relief is available.

A recipient who suspects that an unexpected payment belongs to someone else should verify its source and avoid spending the funds while the position is clarified. Any return payment should be processed through a reliable banking channel. Instructions from an unknown person to send funds to a different third-party account may themselves present a fraud risk.

For businesses offering embedded payments, marketplace balances or wallets, the wider lesson concerns operational design. An error-reporting mechanism, transaction traceability, escalation responsibilities and effective preservation of communication records help reduce both customer harm and evidential uncertainty. These are not substitutes for substantive legal rights, but they may make the difference between an explainable incident and a protracted dispute.

## Conclusion

Digital transfers move value quickly; the legal consequences of an unintended transfer still depend on ownership, obligations and the recipient's conduct. The May 2026 judgment demonstrates that knowingly retaining and misappropriating an erroneous credit can cross the line into criminal liability. It does not establish that every erroneous transfer is a crime or that banks must reverse every reported mistake. Those distinctions are essential for individuals, lenders, banks and technology platforms alike.
