As flexible work arrangements become more common, employers increasingly allow employees to perform their duties outside the office. However, working from home does not necessarily place an employee within a single legal category.

Mongolia's Labour Law separately regulates **home-based employment** under Article 67 and **remote employment** under Article 68. The distinction matters because the statutory requirements concerning the employment agreement, working time and the employee's use of personal equipment are not identical.

## Home-based employment under Article 67

Article 67 permits an employee to perform agreed work from home or another chosen location, under the employer's management and supervision, using equipment or materials provided by the employer or owned by the employee.

In addition to the ordinary terms required for an employment agreement, Article 67.2 requires the parties to address the location and nature of the work, the time and method of delivering completed work, remuneration, relevant unit rates and compensation for the use of the employee's own property or equipment.

Employers using this arrangement should clearly document how performance is assessed, how completed work is accepted and how remuneration is calculated.

Article 67.3 also expressly requires the employer to check the safety of the equipment and tools used by a home-based worker and provide appropriate occupational safety instructions.

## Remote employment under Article 68

Article 68 addresses employment where duties are performed through electronic networks, either permanently or for part of the employee's working arrangements.

This structure may be relevant to software developers, analysts, financial professionals, administrative personnel and other employees whose duties are performed through digital systems.

Under Article 68.2, the employment agreement must specifically address the work location, the time and method for delivering work and compensation for the employee's use of personal property or equipment.

Importantly, an employee working on a laptop from home is not necessarily a home-based worker under Article 67. The correct legal classification depends on the nature of the work and the parties' actual arrangement.

## A significant difference in working-time regulation

Article 67.5 expressly excludes home-based workers under that provision from Chapter Six of the Labour Law, which governs working time and rest periods.

Article 68 does not contain an equivalent general exclusion. Under Article 68.3, remote employees generally enjoy the same rights and bear the same obligations as other employees performing comparable duties, except where the law provides otherwise.

For remote employment, the ordinary working-time framework therefore remains important. Articles 84.1 and 84.2 generally limit normal working time to 40 hours per week and eight hours per day, while Article 91.1 addresses overtime initiated by the employer.

Nevertheless, an email or instruction sent outside normal hours does not automatically establish overtime. Whether additional work was actually performed, at whose initiative and under what contractual conditions remains relevant.

## Equipment, expenses and information protection

Both Articles 67.2 and 68.2 require the parties to agree on compensation where employees use their own property or equipment.

The agreement should therefore specify who provides work equipment, how related costs are allocated, who maintains the equipment and how company information is protected and returned.

This is particularly relevant where employees use personal computers or communication services to access confidential business information.

## Practical implications for employers

A lawful arrangement requires more than labelling an agreement as a remote-work contract.

Employers should examine the work actually performed, the level of management and supervision, the agreed working schedule, reporting arrangements, equipment expenses and performance-assessment procedures.

Where employees divide their time between an office and a remote location, working-time records and communication procedures should also reflect that arrangement.

## Conclusion

Home-based work and remote work both provide flexibility, but Mongolian law regulates them through distinct provisions.

Employers should select and document the appropriate arrangement based on the actual nature of the work, rather than the employee's physical location alone. Particular attention should be given to working time, performance expectations, equipment expenses and occupational safety.
