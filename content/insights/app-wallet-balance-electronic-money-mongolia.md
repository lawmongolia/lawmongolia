Transport, delivery, marketplace and other digital platforms increasingly show customers an in-app “wallet”, “balance”, “credit” or similar account. The functionality may support advance payments, refunds or loyalty incentives. These labels help users navigate the product, but they do not determine the legal classification of the amounts recorded. An electronically displayed balance is not necessarily electronic money. Conversely, labelling it an internal account does not exempt an operator from financial-services regulation if the underlying service is regulated.

The starting point is Mongolia's [Law on the National Payment System](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/12668). Article 5.1.16 defines payment services broadly, article 10 addresses the permissions issued by the Bank of Mongolia, and article 16.1 prohibits the unauthorised provision of payment services and certain associated activities. Article 35 sets out a more specific framework for electronic money. A sound legal analysis should therefore ask two separate questions: does the balance constitute electronic money, and, even if it does not, does the platform carry out another regulated payment activity?

## The statutory characteristics of electronic money

Article 35.2 of the National Payment System Law requires electronic money to possess several characteristics together. Its monetary value is stored electronically and backed by funds; third parties other than the issuer and its own customers accept it in payment; and it is freely convertible into and from monetary funds. Article 35.3 establishes equivalence between one unit of electronic money and one Mongolian tugrik.

A user-funded balance that can be spent across independent merchants, transferred and redeemed in cash raises a materially different regulatory question from a promotional discount point redeemable only against the issuer's own services. These are distinctions about the product's functions rather than the branding chosen by the developer. A closed-loop loyalty point will not necessarily satisfy all the statutory attributes of electronic money, but it is equally unsafe to declare every limited-purpose or prepaid arrangement exempt without examining its real use.

The [Bank of Mongolia's Electronic Money Regulation](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail?lawId=208386) describes payments, transfers, conversion and account-related actions in sections 2.1 and 2.6. Section 2.7 provides that electronic money is issued against funds received from the participant. Section 2.10 states that loans must not be issued using electronic money. These rules reinforce the importance of distinguishing a customer's funded balance from the lender's credit receivable, even when both amounts are shown within a single app.

## A prepaid balance, refund entitlement and loyalty credit are not the same obligation

Consider a platform receiving MNT 100,000 from a customer and recording that sum for future purchases. The existence of a bank transfer and an internal ledger entry alone does not resolve the licensing question. The legal position depends on whether the funds can only be spent on the operator's own service, whether payments can be made to third-party merchants, whether the balance can be transferred to another user and the circumstances in which unused funds can be refunded.

A refund resulting from a cancelled purchase has a different origin from a voluntary top-up. The customer may have a contractual right to repayment. Replacing that repayment with a voucher or non-refundable platform credit cannot be assumed permissible merely because the original payment was made through an app. The purchase contract, refund terms and customer's actual acceptance must be examined.

Mixing prepaid amounts, cash refunds, promotional credits and electronic-money balances into a single technical field also creates accounting and legal difficulties. The operator may be able to display one convenient total to the user, but internally it should still distinguish what each component represents, how it can be spent and who must discharge the associated liability.

## Operating a payment flow may be regulated even without issuing electronic money

Article 5.1.14 of the National Payment System Law defines a payment-service provider by reference to authorisation from the Bank of Mongolia. A platform that collects customer money and settles payments to independent merchants in its own capacity may therefore need an analysis of payment-service activity in addition to electronic-money status.

The outcome can differ when the platform merely provides the technological interface for a separately licensed payment-service provider. In that case, counsel should map which entity receives the customer's payment instruction, which entity holds the money, whose bank account is used and which entity is contractually responsible for settlement, refunds and disputes. Labels such as “aggregator”, “agent” and “technology provider” should correspond to actual operations.

A business also cannot assume that a partner's licence automatically extends to every group company or contractual participant. Article 17 of the National Payment System Law restricts transfers of authorisations and associated rights. The contractual arrangements must therefore be assessed against the particular regulatory permission actually held by the licensed participant.

## The appropriate review begins with a funds-flow diagram

Before launching an in-app wallet, a business should identify the source of every user balance, the terms on which it is credited, the recipients to whom it can be paid and how it is extinguished or returned. This requires a transaction-level funds-flow diagram, not only a summary of the user interface. It should reconcile the bank accounts, internal wallet ledger, merchant settlement instructions, customer terms and refund procedures.

Once the flow is understood, the company can evaluate whether it should seek its own regulatory authorisation, contract with an appropriately licensed provider or maintain a narrower prepaid commercial arrangement. The correct answer depends on substance, not a preferred product label.

## Conclusion

A “wallet” is a product feature, not a self-standing legal exemption or licence category. The receipt, custody, recording, transfer and redemption of funds must be assessed individually. Electronic money has specific statutory characteristics, while other payment functions may be regulated in their own right. Resolving these questions before customers begin loading funds is materially safer than attempting to reclassify the balances after the service is operating.
