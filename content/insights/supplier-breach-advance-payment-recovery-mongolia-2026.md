When a Mongolian company pays a supplier in advance but receives no goods or services by the agreed deadline, requesting a refund may be only one part of the legal response. The buyer should first establish the exact contractual breach, whether the supplier must be given time to cure it, whether withdrawal or termination is available and how any partial performance should be accounted for.

## An advance does not establish completed performance

Article 206.1 of the [Civil Code](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail?lawId=299) sets out the principle that obligations should be performed properly at the agreed place and time. Payment of an advance does not discharge the supplier's separate duty to deliver goods or perform contracted work. The scope of that duty should be proven through the agreement, purchase order, technical specification, payment confirmation and acceptance records.

Consider a company ordering equipment for MNT 100 million and paying MNT 60 million in advance. If delivery never occurs, the buyer should retain the payment evidence, agreed delivery date, product specification and communications establishing non-performance. These amounts are illustrative; Mongolian law does not prescribe a universal 60 per cent advance for such contracts.

## Must additional time be granted before withdrawal?

Article 225.1 of the Civil Code addresses withdrawal following breach and failure to perform within an additional period. Article 225.2 concerns prior demands for performance. The Code also recognises circumstances where an additional period is unnecessary and limitations on withdrawal. Accordingly, late performance does not automatically justify immediate cancellation in every contract.

A formal notice should identify the obligation breached, give a legally appropriate time for performance when required and reserve the remedies that will be pursued if the breach continues. Preserve evidence showing when and how the notice was delivered to the agreed contact address.

## Withdrawal and termination are not identical

Articles 204, 205, 221 and 225 of the Civil Code address different consequences associated with withdrawal and termination. Withdrawal may create obligations to return what the parties have already received and reconcile performances. Termination of an ongoing contract may call for a distinct accounting of work or services properly performed before it ended.

If the buyer accepted part of the goods or identifiable completed services, demanding return of the entire advance without reconciliation may be unfounded. Equally, expenditure claimed by the supplier does not automatically justify retaining an advance. The correct answer depends on performance, the contract and the legally available remedy.

## Contractual penalties and damages

Article 232 of the Civil Code regulates contractual penalties. The daily penalty for delay cannot exceed 0.5 per cent of the value of the unperformed obligation, and the overall penalty is subject to a 50 per cent statutory limit. A penalty requires a valid contractual basis and correct calculation of the breach and its duration; it does not arise automatically. A separate damages claim may require consideration where the legal conditions are met.

Documented additional freight charges, substitute procurement costs or losses arising from a delay may be relevant, but the buyer must establish breach, quantum and causation. Estimated future profits are not a substitute for evidence of recoverable loss.

## Preventing the next dispute

Procurement contracts should specify advance-payment conditions, delivery milestones, objective acceptance criteria, defect-remedy periods, notice channels, repayment deadlines, guarantees and dispute resolution. Staged payments or a suitable bank guarantee may be appropriate for higher-value transactions. These measures cannot prevent every default, but they can considerably improve the company's position if formal recovery becomes necessary.
