The way contracts are formed has changed markedly in recent years. Where
parties once expected to sign the same paper document by hand, users now
express contractual consent by logging into an application, accepting
terms, entering a one-time password, or using an electronic signature.

As a result, the validity of an electronic contract can no longer be
reduced to the question of whether a handwritten signature appears on a
page. The more important questions are whether the contracting person
can be reliably identified, which terms that person accepted and when,
and whether the business can later prove that the accepted version was
not altered.

## Mongolian law recognises electronic contracting
The Civil Code of Mongolia recognises the possibility of entering into
transactions electronically. A contract therefore does not necessarily
have to exist on paper or bear a handwritten signature.

Parties may execute an electronic document using a digital signature.
They may also express and mutually accept their intention through
software, information systems, or other technical means, provided that
the applicable legal requirements for the transaction are satisfied.

This does not mean that electronic form displaces every other statutory
formality. If a particular transaction requires notarisation,
registration, or another special form under law, that requirement
continues to apply.

Two questions should therefore be kept distinct: whether the parties
validly expressed their agreement electronically, and whether the
particular transaction also complied with any additional formalities
imposed by law.

## An OTP should not automatically be treated as a signature
In practice, businesses sometimes assume that entering a one-time
password makes the contract indisputable. That conclusion is too broad.

An OTP is not itself the contract, nor is every OTP automatically
equivalent to a statutory digital signature.

Its evidential value depends on the surrounding authentication process:
how the code was issued, to whom it was delivered, how the user had
previously been identified, which document the confirmation related to,
and how the system recorded and preserved the transaction.

For example, evidence is materially stronger where a user has been
verified through a registered account, reviews a particular version of
the contract, and then confirms acceptance using an OTP sent to a
verified telephone number.

By contrast, an OTP sent to a number that has not been reliably
associated with the user, without preserving the accepted contract
version or transaction logs, may provide far weaker evidence of consent.

The legal value of an OTP therefore lies less in the six-digit code
itself than in the authentication and record-keeping framework behind
it.

## A checkbox is one method of expressing consent
"I agree" checkboxes are common in digital services, but they should not
be treated as conclusive evidence in isolation.

If an unidentified visitor simply ticks a box on a website, it may later
be difficult to establish who performed that action.

The position is stronger where the user signs into a verified account,
has meaningful access to the contractual terms, affirmatively accepts
them, and the system preserves the date, time, user identifier, and
specific version of the terms.

A checkbox is therefore not the contract itself. It is one recorded act
by which the user may express acceptance of the contractual terms.

## The principal challenge is often evidence, not electronic form
Businesses naturally seek to make digital onboarding as simple as
possible. That improves user experience, but excessive simplification
may weaken the evidence available if the transaction is later disputed.

A robust electronic contracting system should be capable of answering
several basic questions. How was the user identified and authenticated?
Which version of the contract was presented? Were important terms such
as price, duration, renewal, payment and liability reasonably available
to the user? When was acceptance recorded? If an OTP was used, where and
when was it sent? Can the business demonstrate that the accepted terms
were not subsequently changed?

If the system does not preserve this information, a business may
eventually be left with little more than an internal statement that its
database shows the user as having accepted the terms.

## Contract version history should be preserved
A common weakness in digital contracting is to maintain only one live
copy of the terms and overwrite it whenever the terms are revised.

That practice may make it impossible to establish which terms a user
accepted several years earlier.

Each version should therefore carry an identifiable version number and
effective date, and the user's acceptance record should be linked
directly to that version.

Where material terms such as price, duration, automatic renewal, payment
obligations or liability are changed, the business should also have a
clear process for notifying users and, where legally or contractually
required, obtaining renewed consent.

## Conclusion
There is no sound basis for treating an electronic contract as
inherently weaker than a paper contract. A well-designed electronic
system may, in fact, create a richer evidential record than a document
bearing only a signature and a date.

Electronic systems can preserve the identity of the account, the exact
time of acceptance, the authentication method, the accepted contract
version and the transaction history.

The quality of an electronic contract should therefore be assessed not
by whether the user clicked a button, but by whether the business can
later prove who contracted, what was accepted and that the accepted
terms remained intact.
