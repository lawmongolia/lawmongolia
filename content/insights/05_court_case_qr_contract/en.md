# Why Did the Court Find a QR-Coded Agreement Insufficient to Prove Contract Formation?

*Case note | Administrative law, electronic contracts and evidence | 14 October 2026*

Does a contract file stored in an electronic system, complete with a QR code, conclusively establish that the parties entered into the agreement? A judgment issued by the Administrative Court of First Instance in Khentii Province on 28 April 2025, No. 127/ШШ/2025/0004, illustrates why the answer depends on the governing legal requirements and the evidence of actual acceptance.

The noteworthy feature of this case is that the court rejected the landholder's explanations for failing to cultivate the land, yet still invalidated the administrative decision cancelling the land possession certificate. The authority had not established when the underlying land possession agreement was concluded and could therefore not prove that the required two-year period had elapsed.

## Background to the dispute

In February 2022, an individual identified in the published judgment as D.M. won an auction for agricultural land in Darkhan soum, Khentii Province. By an administrative order dated 23 March 2022, the individual was allocated approximately 201.8 hectares for irrigated fodder-crop cultivation for a term of 60 years.

On 20 January 2025, the soum governor revoked the land possession certificate on the ground that the land had not been used for its stated purpose for two consecutive years. The landholder challenged that order in the administrative court. While the parties did not materially dispute the absence of cultivation, they disagreed about whether there had been an acceptable justification, whether and when the land possession agreement had been concluded, and when the two-year period could begin to run.

## Two separate requirements had to be established

Article 40.1.6 of Mongolia's Land Law addresses the cancellation of a land possession certificate where land has not been used for its contractual purpose for two consecutive years without an acceptable reason. The court treated the justification for non-use and the length of the relevant period as distinct matters requiring proof.

The landholder referred to difficulties obtaining the land documentation, family-related financial pressures and the impact of severe winter conditions. The court did not accept these circumstances as establishing an adequate justification for failing to use this particular land. It expressly concluded that the land had remained unused without a legally sufficient reason.

That finding did not establish the second requirement. Under the statutory framework concerning possession by agreement and certificate, the date of formation of the relevant land possession agreement mattered to the calculation of the two-year period. The administrative authority still needed evidence showing that the agreement had been formed and when the period started.

## Why the QR code did not resolve the issue

Two versions of a QR-coded land possession agreement bearing the same agreement number were submitted to the court. One was dated 13 December 2022 and the other 20 January 2025. The authority attributed the difference to print dates, but it could not demonstrate when and how the parties had concluded the agreement in 2022.

The records presented a more fundamental problem: the relevant field in the cadastral electronic system indicated that the contractual terms had **not been accepted**. The claimant said that they had scanned the QR code and viewed the document, but evidence of acceptance and confirmation of its terms was not established. The court therefore declined to infer contract formation merely from the existence of the document in the electronic register.

The court also considered the timing of an amendment to Article 23.4.2 of the Land Law, made on 7 December 2023, concerning land agreements and certificates handled through the electronic system. Since the original land allocation order dated from March 2022, it was necessary to examine the rules applicable at the relevant time rather than assume that the later framework proved the earlier agreement had been made.

As the authority could not prove the agreement's formation date, it could not establish the commencement and expiry of the two-year non-use period. The court upheld the claim and annulled the governor's order of 20 January 2025, No. A/06.

## What this does—and does not—mean for electronic contracts

It would be inaccurate to treat this judgment as a general rule that electronic agreements or QR-coded documents are invalid. The court assessed the particular land law provisions, the timing of the applicable rules and conflicting evidence about acceptance. A contract governed by different statutory formalities or supported by different records might produce a different outcome.

The broader lesson is evidential. Creating a PDF, assigning an agreement number, recording that a user opened a document or generating a QR code may not, by themselves, establish that the relevant party accepted the contractual terms. Where formation is disputed, an organisation may need to demonstrate who accepted which version of the agreement, through what legally effective process, and at what time.

For businesses operating digital platforms, electronic onboarding or online contracting systems, it is therefore prudent to design the contracting workflow and the evidence-retention workflow together. In addition to meeting any mandatory formal requirements, a system should be capable of preserving the agreed version of the terms, the timing and attribution of acceptance, and a reliable audit record capable of being produced in a dispute.

These are practical risk-management observations drawn from the case, not a claim that the judgment created a universal technical checklist for electronic contracts. The precise legal formalities and available methods of proof must be assessed in the context of each transaction.

## Conclusion

The cancellation order was not set aside because the court accepted that the landholder had a compelling excuse for failing to cultivate the land. The decisive evidential deficiency was that the authority did not prove when the land possession agreement had been concluded, leaving the statutory two-year period unestablished.

For organisations using electronic agreements, this case illustrates the difference between retaining a contractual document and retaining evidence that the agreement was actually formed.
