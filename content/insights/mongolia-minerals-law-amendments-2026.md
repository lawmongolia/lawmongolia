On 26 May 2026 the Government of Mongolia submitted to the State Great Khural a bill amending the Minerals Law. The bill contains a number of substantial changes affecting exploration, extraction, processing, mine closure, rehabilitation, community involvement and licence fees.

The Khural began the first reading in June 2026 but postponed the vote on 50 amendments where positions differed. As at September 2026, everything below should therefore be read as a bill under consideration, not as law in force.

## Processing plants would require a licence

One of the most significant changes in the bill is a licensing requirement for the operation of processing plants.

Under the bill, a person operating a processing plant independently, without an extraction licence, would need to obtain a licence. It would be granted for five years and could be extended for the same period. Where the holder of an extraction licence carries out processing as part of its own operations, a separate processing licence would not be required.

Obtaining the licence would require a feasibility study, a detailed environmental impact assessment and a guaranteed water supply, among other requirements. The bill would also require the feasibility study to be updated where the capacity, equipment or technology of the plant changes.

If adopted, this change would materially alter the legal basis on which companies that process ore and concentrate without holding an extraction licence operate.

## Mine closure costs would have to be funded in advance

The current mining regime already imposes rehabilitation and closure obligations. The bill would establish a more detailed system for securing those obligations financially and in advance.

Under the bill, a closure and rehabilitation plan would have to be prepared before a mine or processing plant opens, updated every three to five years, and finalised no less than three years before closure.

Beyond that, the money needed for closure, rehabilitation and post-closure monitoring would have to be accumulated in advance. Funding would have to be fully in place within the first two thirds of the mine's operating life; a mine or plant with an operating life of up to three years would have to deposit its closure funding in full in the first year.

If adopted, a company running a mining project would need to reflect closure costs in its feasibility study and financial model from the start of operations rather than at the end of the project.

## A new route to an exploration licence

The bill introduces a new arrangement under which a person that has carried out state geological survey and prospecting work under contract could, on meeting certain conditions, apply for an exploration licence over part of the contract area.

A person that has fully performed the contracted work and delivered its reports, primary materials and samples to the state database could apply for an exploration licence over no more than one fifth of the contract area. Such a licence would be granted for two years and could be extended once for a further two years.

This would create an additional route to exploration rights, linked to the results of state geological work rather than solely to a competitive tender.

## Obligations towards local communities would be more clearly defined

The bill reinforces the principle that information on the activities of exploration, extraction and processing licence holders should be publicly available.

It would also require a licence holder to conclude an agreement with the governor of the soum or district on environmental protection, the development of infrastructure connected with the mine or plant, and increasing employment, and to submit that agreement to the governor of the aimag or capital city.

If adopted, an agreement with the local community would need to be treated as part of the legal compliance of the mine rather than as a general corporate social responsibility document.

## Licence fees could change each year

The bill provides for exploration and extraction licence fees to be indexed to the increase in the consumer price index over the previous year.

If adopted, licence fees would no longer stay at a fixed amount over many years but could rise with inflation. The financial plan for a long-term mining project would therefore need to allow for future increases in licence fees.

## What mining companies should be doing now

Because the bill has not been adopted, companies are not obliged to bring their operations into line with it.

That said, companies operating processing plants, planning a new mining project, or running a project with high closure costs would do well to follow the parliamentary process closely. Pre-funding of closure costs bears directly on project cash flow, and licensing for processing plants could affect the permit structure and contracts of a plant already in operation.

## Conclusion

The 2026 bill amending the Minerals Law is significant because it would change more than the granting of mining licences. Processing plants, mine closure, financial security, community involvement and transparency requirements would all be reworked at once.

However, many amendments were tabled during the first reading and the final wording may change. The provisions of the bill should not be treated as law in force when drafting contracts, making investment decisions or preparing budgets.
