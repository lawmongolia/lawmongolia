Companies entering exclusive distribution arrangements with foreign counterparties often include a clause requiring one party to sell only the counterparty's products, backed by a penalty for breach. The difficult question is how large that penalty can lawfully be, and how Mongolian courts review it.

## Parties cannot fix any penalty amount they choose

Article 232 of the Civil Code permits a contractual penalty to secure performance of an obligation, but this is not an unlimited right.

The daily penalty for delay may not exceed 0.5 per cent of the value of the unperformed obligation, and the aggregate penalty may not exceed 50 per cent of that value. These caps apply equally to exclusive and distribution agreements, not only to ordinary domestic commercial contracts.

A clause providing "5 per cent per month" or "60 per cent per annum" will, if enforced, risk being reduced to the statutory ceiling when a Mongolian court is asked to apply it.

## Translating a foreign template agreement carries real risk

In practice, many companies adopt a foreign parent's or supplier's standard template, translating only the language, names and addresses. Such templates often include concepts common in US or English law, including "liquidated damages" clauses stating that the specified amount is not a penalty.

This framing does not necessarily work under Mongolian law. Labelling a clause "liquidated damages, not a penalty" does not, by itself, exempt it from the statutory cap. A Mongolian court is likely to look at the substance of the payment rather than the label the parties gave it, and to apply the statutory penalty limits accordingly.

A foreign template should therefore not simply be translated and signed. The penalty clause needs to be reworked to align specifically with article 232 of the Civil Code.

## Where the penalty is insufficient, actual loss may still be claimed

The statutory cap on penalties does not mean that the injured party is limited to the penalty amount and nothing more.

Article 229 of the Civil Code distinguishes actual loss from lost profit, both of which are recoverable in full. Actual damage and lost profit exceeding the penalty, and evidenced separately, may be claimed in addition.

To preserve that right, the contract should expressly reserve the ability to claim actual loss alongside the penalty. Some foreign templates state that liquidated damages are "the sole and exclusive remedy" — how such a clause would be interpreted under Mongolian law needs to be checked carefully before the contract is signed.

## Practical steps for businesses

When entering an exclusive or distribution agreement, check the penalty rate and cap against article 232 of the Civil Code, rework any foreign "liquidated damages" clause to align with Mongolian terminology and rules, expressly preserve the right to claim actual loss in addition to the penalty, and specify governing law and dispute resolution clearly, with an understanding of how a Mongolian court would treat the penalty if the matter came before it.

## Conclusion

Including a penalty clause in an exclusive distribution agreement is standard practice, but setting it above the statutory ceiling risks it not being enforced in full. Rather than translating a foreign standard template, drafting the penalty clause specifically to align with Mongolia's Civil Code is the more reliable way to protect the arrangement.
