Where a government authority refuses an individual's or company's application, withdraws a permit or restricts a legal right, the general rule may allow an administrative appeal **within 30 days after the decision has been duly notified**. However, the time limit for a complaint **to an administrative authority** and the deadline for a claim **before an administrative court** can arise at **different stages**. The first question is whether a sector-specific law supplies a different route or deadline. The relevant general provisions are [Articles 92–94 of the General Administrative Law](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/11259) and [Article 14 of the Law on Administrative Litigation](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/11703).

## Which government decisions amount to administrative acts?

An administrative act is a form of decision by an administrative authority in the sphere of public law that directly affects the rights or obligations of an identifiable person or entity. For example, a refusal to issue a licence or a decision revoking an existing permit may require examination as an administrative act, depending on its legal effect and the authority involved.

Not every letter or informational response from a government body is necessarily an administrative act. It is important to establish whether the communication produces a direct legal consequence, whether the authority was legally empowered to make it and whether the resulting dispute belongs before an administrative court. Tax, public procurement, non-bank financial services, licensing and other regulated sectors may have special review procedures. The general 30-day rule must not be applied to every such dispute without checking the applicable statute. The separate procedure for contested tax assessments is explained in [this article on tax appeals](/en/insights/challenging-tax-assessments-mongolia-prepayment-appeal).

## Is time counted from the decision date or from notification?

Article 94.1 of the [General Administrative Law](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/11259) provides for an administrative complaint within **30 days after the administrative act was notified** under the prescribed procedure. The date printed on the face of the act may differ from the date when the affected person was duly notified.

Under Article 43, the issuing administrative body is responsible for notification. Notably, **Article 43.8 puts the burden of proving the date of notification on that authority** if notification is disputed. Evidence such as a signed delivery record, official electronic-system message, postal receipt or email transmission record can therefore become decisive. The calculation of the final day must also reflect the applicable rules governing statutory periods and any special legislation.

## Which authority should receive the administrative complaint?

Article 93.1 generally directs the complainant to the **higher administrative authority** above the body that issued the act or to an authority assigned the function of reviewing complaints. Under Article 93.2, where no such reviewing authority exists, the complaint may be submitted to the authority that issued the original act.

A useful complaint identifies the challenged decision and its date, the legal right affected, the disputed findings of fact or law and the outcome sought. If, for example, a licensing refusal ignored an application document already submitted on time, the applicant should identify that document, the date of its delivery and the error in the authority's stated reasons. Keep confirmation that the complaint was received, together with its registration number or other record.

## How quickly must the authority respond?

Under Article 94.3, unless another law provides otherwise, the authority must review and respond to a complaint **within 30 days**. Under Article 94.4, where necessary, the responsible senior official may extend that period **by up to another 30 days** and must notify the complainant. The absence of a response exactly 30 days after submission does not always establish delay; a valid extension must be checked.

Article 99.1 requires the authority to notify the complainant of the decision on the complaint, in writing or electronically, **within five working days after that decision was made**, and to document notification. The notification date of a decision rejecting an administrative complaint may then become relevant to the separate judicial filing deadline. It is therefore important to retain both the decision and proof of when it was communicated.

## When does the 30-day deadline for judicial review start?

Under [Article 14.1 of the Law on Administrative Litigation](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/11703), where the ordinary administrative complaint procedure has been followed and no special law provides otherwise, the claim generally must be filed **within 30 days of notification of the reviewing authority's decision** in the situations covered by that provision. Article 14.2 addresses a different situation: if the complaint is not decided within the legally prescribed period, court proceedings must generally be commenced **within 30 days after that period expires**.

Article 14.3 deals with cases where another law provides for direct recourse to court. The specific statutory deadline applies, or 30 days from notification if no special period is specified. Article 14.4 provides for **direct court proceedings within 30 days of notification** if there is no higher authority or body with the relevant complaint-review function. These routes cannot be collapsed into a single universal rule.

There are also express exceptions. Article **14.5** excludes particular claims, including claims seeking a declaration that an administrative act is manifestly unlawful, certain questions of the existence of a legal relationship and challenges concerning administrative normative acts, from the deadlines in Articles 14.1–14.4. This is **not** permission to relabel any late challenge as a “manifestly unlawful” act. The claim's actual subject matter, the specific legal grounds and the court's jurisdiction still have to be established.

## Does failure to hear the affected person invalidate the decision?

Under **Article 26.1 of the General Administrative Law**, an authority generally must give a person whose rights or legitimate interests may be affected an opportunity to express views and explanations about material circumstances before issuing the act. Article 27 deals with notification, methods of hearing and documentation. **Article 28**, however, sets out situations in which a hearing may be omitted.

Accordingly, an allegation that the authority “did not hear me” needs to be evaluated against the applicable statutory exception, the actual procedure followed and the effect of the irregularity. Articles 40.2.3 and 40.2.5 further require a written act to set out its factual and legal grounds and identify the body and time limit for challenging it. A refusal that merely cites an internal policy without explaining its legal basis may warrant closer examination, but the legal result depends on the circumstances.

## Can a late complaint be restored, and does an appeal suspend the act?

Article 94.2 of the General Administrative Law provides a mechanism to seek restoration of an administrative complaint period missed for a **compelling justified reason**, through an application submitted **within three months after the expiry** of that period. The reason must be supported by evidence; an extension does not take effect automatically. The procedural rules applicable to judicial claims must be examined separately.

Filing a complaint also does **not automatically suspend enforcement** of the challenged administrative act. Where suspension is necessary, a separate request for relief must be assessed under the relevant legislation and procedural rules. Treating an administrative complaint as an automatic stay can create significant practical risk if the challenged decision continues to operate.
