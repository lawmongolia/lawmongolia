**Legal due diligence before buying a company in Mongolia** is not simply a review of the seller's presentation or corporate registration certificate. A buyer must understand precisely what it is acquiring, which obligations remain with the target business, whether ownership can lawfully be transferred and which matters could reduce the value of the investment after completion. A profitable business may still have material exposure from disputed taxes, encumbered assets, weak software ownership, unrecorded guarantees or contracts that terminate when control changes.

The analysis typically draws on the [Company Law of Mongolia](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/310), the [Law on State Registration of Legal Entities](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/13591), sector-specific requirements and the target's commercial documentation. A due diligence report should help the buyer make four decisions: **whether to proceed, what price is justified, what must be resolved before closing and which contractual protections to negotiate**.

## 1. Share acquisition or asset acquisition?

In a **share deal**, the shares in the target company are transferred. The company generally continues to hold its assets and to be bound by its existing debts, contracts and obligations. The buyer obtains an interest in that legal entity rather than direct title to each asset that it owns.

In an **asset deal**, selected assets and, where agreed and legally possible, specified rights or contractual positions are transferred. This can involve separate consents, registrations, taxes and employee-related questions. It may also make individual permits or contracts particularly difficult to move.

Article 3.2 of the [Company Law](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/310) makes an important distinction: shares evidence participation in the company's capital, not personal ownership of each asset of the company. The choice of transaction structure should therefore come before detailed drafting of the purchase agreement.

## 2. Confirm the seller's title and the right to transfer shares

The buyer should reconcile the state registration information, company charter, shareholder records, historic share transfers, evidence of prior consideration and any pledges or pending claims. The fact that a person acts as a director or negotiates the sale does not by itself prove that person owns all the shares being offered.

For Mongolian limited liability companies, [Articles 5.3–5.9 of the Company Law](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/310) address pre-emptive rights and the procedure for proposing a sale of shares. The charter may alter the default position. A third-party buyer therefore needs to check **the charter, the relevant notices and evidence that applicable rights have been respected** before treating the transaction as ready to close.

Ownership should also be distinguished from beneficial ownership and control. Complex holding structures require an examination of the chain of entities and whether the individuals presented as the ultimate owners are supported by reliable documents.

## 3. Look beyond reported financial liabilities

Financial statements are necessary but rarely sufficient. Legal due diligence should examine financing agreements, shareholder loans, security interests, third-party guarantees, pending claims, unpaid suppliers, tax assessments, advance payments subject to refund and potential contractual penalties.

A company may have entered into a guarantee that has not produced an immediate cash outflow. A supplier dispute may not yet have resulted in litigation. A major customer may be seeking reimbursement for incomplete services. Those matters can be significant even if the latest financial report appears healthy.

The findings should distinguish **confirmed liabilities, contingent exposures, disputed obligations and risks that cannot be quantified because evidence is missing**. A statement that the seller has not provided records is not evidence that no liability exists.

## 4. Does a change of control affect key contracts?

Lenders, landlords, distributors, platform operators, technology licensors and major customers may negotiate *change-of-control* provisions. These can require consent, notification, accelerated repayment, repricing or termination when control over a contracting entity changes.

The actual contract wording matters. Not every share transfer triggers a change-of-control clause, and not every clause has the same consequence. Counsel should identify the trigger, threshold, notice period, decision-maker and remedy under each material agreement.

If one customer represents most of the target's revenue, the risk of losing that agreement may be more significant than several minor corporate-record deficiencies combined. Due diligence should therefore prioritise contracts by commercial dependence, not simply by document length or nominal value.

## 5. Verify licences and regulatory status

Corporate registration does not prove that the target has every approval needed for its regulated operations. In sectors such as financial services, technology-enabled payments, transport, healthcare and mining, the review should cover licence holder, permitted activities, expiry, restrictions, reporting duties, regulatory correspondence, inspections and potential sanctions.

The [Law on Permits and Notifications](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/16530780109311) and applicable sector legislation must be considered together. A licence should not be assumed to transfer automatically with an asset purchase. In a share deal, the licensed entity may remain the same, yet a change in ownership or control may still be subject to specific requirements. These distinctions can determine whether completion conditions are needed.

## 6. Examine employment and management exposure

The legal review should look at employment contracts, unpaid wages and incentives, annual-leave liabilities, confidentiality obligations, employment disputes, social insurance compliance and arrangements involving key executives. An acquisition may also raise concerns about retention of employees responsible for technology, clients or regulated functions.

Article 80.3 of the [Labour Law](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/16230709635751) provides that changes in the organisation's affiliation, ownership type, legal form or management are not, unless otherwise provided by law, grounds in themselves for terminating employment. A buyer should not assume it can simply dismiss all staff after purchasing shares. The target's employment obligations and the intended post-acquisition operating model require separate assessment.

## 7. Does the target actually control its intellectual property and data?

For technology businesses, the key assets may be software, data, trademarks, domain names and customer contracts rather than physical property. Yet the target may be using a vendor-controlled repository, commercially restricted open-source components or a third-party platform under a non-transferable licence.

Counsel should trace the ownership and licensing chain for software, source code, databases, branding and other commercially important assets. The [analysis of software ownership in Mongolia](/insights/software-copyright-employee-contractor-client-mongolia-2026) explains why authorship, exclusive exploitation rights and access to code must be distinguished.

Customer data should also be reviewed against the [Law on Personal Data Protection](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/16390288615991), including the purposes for which information was collected, the role of external processors and the arrangements for international transfers. A valuable customer database is not necessarily a legally unrestricted asset.

## 8. Consider transaction approvals and conflicts of interest

The buyer and seller may each need corporate approvals. The [Company Law's Articles 87–88](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/310) regulate major transactions; Articles 89–92 address transactions involving conflicts of interest. The rules must be applied to the actual contracting entity and relevant transaction, not mechanically to every sale of shares.

Review board and shareholder authority, powers of attorney, the articles of association and whether interested persons should abstain. A signature from a senior manager is not a substitute for a corporate decision where one is legally required.

## 9. Convert findings into enforceable purchase terms

A due diligence report creates value only when its findings affect the transaction. Depending on the risks, the share purchase agreement may need conditions precedent, seller warranties, specific indemnities, price adjustments, holdbacks or escrow arrangements, post-closing undertakings and carefully defined remedies.

For example, a missing regulatory consent may be better handled as a condition to closing than as a claim for damages after the buyer has already paid. A quantifiable disputed liability may justify a dedicated indemnity or an agreed amount held back pending resolution. These mechanisms do not arise automatically: **their scope, triggers, evidence and limits must be negotiated**.

The agreement should also define which disclosures qualify warranties, how claims are notified, which documents are delivered at closing and who bears the cost of rectifying known deficiencies.

## 10. What should the final due diligence report contain?

A useful report separates high-priority decision items from administrative observations. For each finding it should state: the facts and documents reviewed, applicable legal rule, likely commercial effect, unresolved evidence, recommendation and whether action is needed before or after completion.

A **red flag** is not necessarily a reason to abandon the transaction. It is a reason to decide deliberately whether to cure the problem, adjust the price, allocate the risk or decline the deal. Where documents have not been supplied, the report should say so expressly rather than assuming compliance.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a company registry extract enough?** No. It assists with verifying registered information but does not establish the absence of tax liabilities, disputed contracts, litigation, defective IP title or regulatory issues.

**Is legal due diligence the same as a financial audit?** No. Legal, financial, tax and technical reviews address different questions, though their findings should inform a single investment decision.

**Does the target automatically lose licences on a share sale?** Not as a universal rule. The licensed entity, specific licence conditions and applicable sector law must be checked.

## Conclusion

A properly scoped acquisition review asks **who owns the shares, what liabilities the buyer will economically bear, what approvals are required and how the negotiated agreement will allocate the remaining risk**. The answer should be supported by documents, not merely the seller's assurances.
