When a supplier fails to deliver, a customer withholds payment or an IT service falls short of its agreed scope, the most useful first step is not necessarily to file a lawsuit. A business should first establish **the legal basis of its claim, the amount sought, the relevant deadlines and the evidence supporting each component**. Delay can mean losing electronic correspondence, relying on inconsistent calculations or missing a limitation period.

Under Mongolian civil procedure, the claimant is responsible for proving the factual basis of its claim, while the defendant must substantiate its objections. Effective **commercial dispute preparation** therefore begins with records created and retained during performance, not a last-minute search for documents after proceedings start.

## 1. A signed contract is only the starting point

The signed agreement is essential, but performance is often established through schedules, amendments, purchase orders, invoices, bank records, delivery or acceptance certificates, emails, incident reports and defect logs.

The first review should identify the controlling contract version, effective amendments, signatory authority, performance deadlines and documents that trigger payment. It should also establish whether the other party accepted the work and whether defects were reported within the applicable contractual or legal period.

Separate the company's own accounting reports from documents acknowledged by the counterparty. A unilateral spreadsheet may explain how a claim was calculated, but it may not establish that the debtor admitted the amount. The same distinction applies to internal incident notes versus records that objectively demonstrate what occurred.

## 2. Link each remedy to its legal basis and evidence

[Article 25.2.2 of Mongolia's Civil Procedure Law](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/302) requires parties to prove the grounds for their claims and objections and to collect and submit relevant evidence. Article 37 governs evidence and means of proof.

A useful internal working document is a **claim–legal basis–evidence matrix**:

| Proposed claim | Legal or contractual basis | Principal records |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outstanding principal | Performance completed; payment became due | Agreement, acceptance record, invoice, bank statement |
| Contractual delay penalty | Relevant penalty term applies and the deadline was missed | Contract clause, date calculation, payment history |
| Defective goods or services | Agreed quality or scope was not met | Specification, acceptance records, complaint, photographs, inspection reports |
| Damages | A breach caused provable loss | Expense records, financial documents and expert analysis where needed |

This matrix is a management tool, not a prescribed court pleading. It helps identify unsupported claims and missing documents before a demand is sent or proceedings are initiated.

## 3. Preserve emails, messages and system logs properly

Modern business transactions leave digital records of orders, fulfilment, handover, incidents and customer complaints. A screenshot alone may not preserve the context required to evaluate a dispute. Where lawful and feasible, retain the **original record, the time it was created, its source and relevant change history**.

For emails, preserve original messages, headers, sender and recipient details, timestamps and attachments. In API or SaaS disputes, request IDs, status and error codes, server time zones, monitoring results and support tickets may matter. Records should be collected without unnecessarily exposing trade secrets or unrelated individuals' personal data.

[Article 44.5 of the Civil Procedure Law](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/302) refers to Article 38 of the Law on Archives and Records Management when evaluating electronic documents. Civil Procedure Law Articles 38.11–38.12 also address disputes concerning the authenticity of certain electronically signed documents and the possible role of expert examination.

The goal is not to assume that technical logs guarantee success in court. It is to preserve their **origin, integrity, timing and context** so their relevance and reliability can later be assessed.

## 4. Act promptly where evidence may disappear

System logs may be deleted automatically after a retention period. Employee departures, changes of hosting provider, scheduled deletion of records or replacement of error logs can also compromise evidence.

Where appropriate, issue an internal **legal hold** identifying relevant records, custodians, storage locations and preservation responsibilities. Keep track of when copies were made and how they relate to original records. Preserving evidence does not authorise unauthorised access to another party's system or unlawful collection of personal data.

[Article 50 of the Civil Procedure Law](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/302) provides a legal mechanism for securing evidence in circumstances involving a risk of change, destruction or concealment. Whether and how to apply for such measures depends on the procedural stage, the requested evidence and the reasons showing a need for preservation.

## 5. Calculate the limitation period early

A promise to pay, ongoing discussions or a creditor's demand letter does not automatically eliminate the risk of limitation. Under [Article 75.2.1 of the Civil Code](https://legalinfo.mn/mn/detail/299), claims arising from contractual obligations are **generally subject to a three-year limitation period**. Certain claims relating to immovable property and claims governed by specific legislation can be subject to different periods.

Establish when the obligation was due, when it was breached and when the right to claim arose. Articles 76, 79 and 82 of the Civil Code contain provisions concerning commencement, interruption and consequences of expiry of limitation periods.

Sending an invoice or demand **does not, by itself, invariably interrupt limitation**. The debtor's subsequent conduct, any acknowledgment, partial payment or relevant proceedings must be assessed on the facts and under the governing rules. Negotiations should never be allowed to continue without monitoring the deadline for commencing proceedings.

## 6. What should a formal demand contain?

Check whether a pre-action demand is mandatory under a particular law or under the contract's dispute-resolution procedure. There is no single mandatory pre-action notice applicable in exactly the same way to every commercial dispute. Nevertheless, a precise written demand can support negotiations and create a clearer record of the parties' positions.

An effective demand identifies the agreement, the obligation, the relevant breach, dates, supporting documents, calculation of amounts, a reasonable deadline for performance, the response channel and potential next steps if the demand is not satisfied.

Avoid presenting principal, contractual penalties and damages as one unexplained total. The counterparty should be able to understand what is being requested, why and by when.

Preserve proof of delivery. For a paper notice, keep receipt or delivery records; for email, keep the relevant message, address and delivery evidence in light of the agreed notice clause. If a response follows, distinguish an acknowledgment of debt from a partial dispute or a complete rejection of liability.

## 7. A seven-step business preparation process

1. Identify the **final effective versions** of the agreement, schedules and amendments.
2. Record each material performance deadline, payment and acceptance event.
3. Match every proposed claim to a contractual or statutory basis and supporting evidence.
4. Lawfully preserve transient materials such as email, logs, transaction records, acceptance reports and defect evidence.
5. Check limitation periods, court or arbitration jurisdiction and mandatory notice requirements.
6. Compare negotiation, settlement, demand and litigation options by risk, cost and likely practical outcome.
7. Before filing, reconcile the claim amount, calculations and documentary bundle so they form a coherent case.

This is a preliminary internal process. Formal evidence collection, expert examinations and court representation may require additional case-specific steps.

## 8. Consider settlement before committing to litigation

Litigation is not always the most valuable commercial outcome. A revised payment schedule, defect-correction programme, partial settlement, additional security or final reconciliation may protect cash flow and preserve a commercially useful relationship.

The settlement document should identify **what is admitted, what is released, what obligations remain and what happens if the revised arrangement is breached**. It should also clarify whether the new agreement replaces the former obligation or merely modifies the time for performance.

Participating in negotiations does not, without a legally relevant event, automatically preserve the right to sue or extend the limitation period. Maintain a separate deadline register throughout the discussions.

## Conclusion

**The quality of evidence matters as much as the value of the claim in commercial litigation.** A business that connects each remedy to the governing agreement, evidence of performance, payment records, communications and applicable deadlines can assess its options earlier. A poorly documented claim, by contrast, may be difficult to enforce even when the underlying commercial grievance is legitimate.
